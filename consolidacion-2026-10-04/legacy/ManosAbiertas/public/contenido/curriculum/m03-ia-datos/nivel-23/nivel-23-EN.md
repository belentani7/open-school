## 📚 Main lesson
Welcome to the Fruit band, the band of the ripe fruit. In Levels 21 and 22 we cleaned the data and learned to talk with it thanks to the AI. Today we turn the findings into images that are understood at a glance: this is visualising, the stage of the data cycle we met in Level 13 and that we already touched on in Level 12 with dashboards.

The first rule: the chart is chosen according to the question. Do you want to compare categories? Use bars. Do you want to see change over time? Use lines. Do you want to show the parts of a whole? Use slices. It is like choosing the tool in the kitchen: you do not cut bread with a ladle. If the chart does not match the question, it confuses instead of clarifying.

The bar chart is the simplest and the most honest: each rectangle is a category and its height, the value. The line chart shows the journey of the data over time, like the temperature of the town from Level 14 or the electricity bill. The pie chart is pretty, but careful: it only works when we want to divide a total into parts. With more than five parts, it becomes unreadable.

Second rule: less is more. A clean chart with a clear title and few elements is worth more than one overloaded with colours and decorations. Every element you add must have a reason. If a colour means nothing, remove it. The reader must understand the image in seconds, not study it like a treasure map.

Third rule: visual honesty. We already met it in Level 12 with deceptive axes. An axis can deceive without lying: if it starts at 300 instead of zero, a small change looks enormous. The person who draws the chart chooses the scale, and that choice is an ethical decision. The golden rule: the image must tell the same truth as the numbers.

Fourth rule: read with judgement. Before trusting a chart, look at four things: the title, the axes, the source and the date. Where did this data come from? Who published it and when? A chart without a source is a suspicious chart. This habit, which we already practised with the open data of Level 14, is the same one we use to read the charts in the news.

Fifth rule: the image does not speak by itself. A chart needs words: the narrative with data. You are the one who says what the image shows, what it means and what it recommends. It is like a photograph in an album: without the family's story, it says nothing. Professionals call it storytelling with data: the chart is the photo and you are the story.

In the final project, the deliverable (report or dashboard) is made of three to five well-chosen visualisations. Each one answers a part of the question we asked in Level 20. You do not need twenty charts: you need the right ones, the ones that tell the story of your project. A Looker Studio dashboard from Level 12 can be the perfect showcase.

For an immigrant, knowing how to read and draw charts is a skill that opens doors. Explaining to the family with a chart where the money goes, presenting a well-drawn survey at the association, or showing a change with a trend line in a complaint: when data looks like an image, people understand it and listen.

And an ethical warning we already know from Level 12: do not use the chart to win the argument, use it to tell the truth. An honest chart can confirm or disprove; the trick of the cut axis or the strange scale can deceive even the person who draws it. Visual honesty is the signature of a good data analyst.

In Level 24 we will change topic: we will learn to protect the data that cost us so much work to collect, clean, analyse and draw. Because data is worth what its security is worth. For now, spend this level turning your findings into clear, honest and well-told images. The fruit is ripe: it is time to show it.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Rosa's budget
Rosa draws a pie chart with the breakdown of her monthly budget. The family sees it at a glance and decides together where to cut back: the narrative with data becomes a kitchen conversation.

### Example 2: Carmen's temperature
Carmen draws a trend line (Level 16) with 20 years of summers in the town. The chart shows the rise and she presents it at the town hall: with the image, the complaint is heard.

### Example 3: Juan's survey
Juan draws bars with the use of the bus by time slots. At the neighbours' assembly, the chart answers questions before they are asked: a well-made visualisation moves the debate forward.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open your final project and decide which finding you want to show first.
Step 2. Choose the type of chart according to the question: bars, lines or slices.
Step 3. Create the chart in Google Sheets or Looker Studio with the clean data from Level 21.
Step 4. Add a clear title and names to the axes.
Step 5. Check the honesty: does the scale deceive? Does it start at zero where it should?
Step 6. Write in two lines what the chart says and what you recommend.
Step 7. Show it to someone you trust and ask: "what do you understand?".
Step 8. Adjust the chart with what you learned and save it in your deliverable.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Which chart shows change over time? a) Bars. b) Lines. c) Slices.
2. Which chart shows the parts of a whole? a) Slices. b) Lines. c) Columns.
3. An axis that starts at a high number instead of zero... a) Is always better. b) Can exaggerate the difference and deceive. c) It does not matter.
4. What makes a chart honest: a) Pretty colours. b) A clear title, clear axes and a source. c) Being very big.
5. The image by itself... a) Speaks for itself. b) Needs your explanation in words. c) Does not need a title.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- A chart can deceive without lying: the scale, the cut of the axis and the selection of data are ethical decisions.
- Do not choose the chart that "looks better": choose the one that tells the truth.
- Cite the source and the date: a chart without origin does not deserve trust.
- If the result is not liked, it is not touched up: it is explained honestly.
- Think of who will read the chart: clarity is a form of justice (Level 19).
