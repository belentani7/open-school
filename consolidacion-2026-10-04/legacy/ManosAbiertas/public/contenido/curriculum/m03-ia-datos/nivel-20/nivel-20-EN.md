## Language: EN · Difficulty: Canopy

## 📚 Main lesson
Welcome to the Canopy band, the highest one in our knowledge tree. The moment of truth has arrived: you are going to be the owner of your own data analysis project, from start to finish. It is not an exam with answers that already exist: it is a journey where you choose the destination, the route and the car. In this level we kick off the band with the most important part: choosing the project well and planning it.

Remember the lesson from Level 13: think backwards. The project does not start with the data; it starts with the question. What do you want to know? The best research question has three qualities. First, it truly matters to you: if it does not interest you, you will not finish it. Second, it can be answered with data that exists or that you can get: there is no point in asking something that cannot be measured. Third, it is concrete: "how is my neighbourhood?" is too big; "how many buses pass my stop between 7 and 9?" is perfect.

Bad questions are the number one cause of failed projects. "Is public transport good?" cannot be measured directly: "good" is an opinion. But "how many buses arrive on time during rush hour?" can be measured, counted and drawn. The rule for writing a good one: if you can count or measure the answer, it is a good question. If you can only give an opinion, rewrite it.

After the question comes the scope. The scope is the boundary of the project: what is in and what is out. A small, finished project is worth more than a big, abandoned one. "I will study the punctuality of line 12 during rush hour for one week" is a perfect scope. "I will study all the transport in my city for a year" is a recipe for failure. Professionals say it in one sentence: "the scope is cut, not stretched." Better a small question answered rigorously than a huge one answered badly.

Third element of the plan: the deliverable. What will your finished project look like? It can be a one-page report, a Looker Studio dashboard (Level 12), a presentation or a poster. Decide the deliverable from the beginning, because it shapes all the work: if the deliverable is a dashboard, you will think in charts from day one; if it is a report, you will think in writing. Knowing where you are going is what lets you choose the road.

Fourth: the schedule. A project without dates is a project that drags on forever. Put a calendar with the five stages of the cycle from Level 13: a week to collect, a couple of days to clean, a week to analyse and visualise, a couple of days to write. You do not need to be strict to the minute: it is enough to be clear about what is done each week and which deliverable marks the end of each stage.

Now the emotional part of this level: choosing the topic. Let me offer you a golden rule for the final project: choose something from YOUR life. The most memorable projects in this course will be the ones that touch what you know: the household spending, the medicines from the neighbourhood pharmacy, the building's water, the bus timetables, the temperatures of your town (Level 14). When the topic is yours, the data becomes personal, and the work becomes something that matters to you.

And one ethical warning to start with, which we already know from Level 19: respect other people's data. If your project uses other people's data (a survey of your neighbours), ask for permission, do not keep more than necessary and do not tell things that identify anyone. An ethical project is not a limited project: it is a project you can present with your head held high.

In the next level we will work on the second great challenge: presenting the findings. Because once you know the answer to your question, you will have to tell it to someone who has not taken the course with you. The science of data has one last stage that almost no one practises well: communicating. For now, spend this level choosing your question, cutting your scope, deciding your deliverable and setting your calendar. The Canopy starts with a good seed: yours.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Household spending
Question: "what takes up most of my household's monthly budget?" Scope: one month of shopping. Deliverable: a one-page report with three charts.

### Example 2: The neighbourhood bus
Question: "how many minutes of delay does line 12 accumulate during rush hour?" Scope: one working week. Deliverable: a Looker Studio dashboard.

### Example 3: The town's temperatures
Question: "how has the average July temperature in my town changed over 20 years?" Scope: the open data from Level 14. Deliverable: a trend line with its interpretation.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open a new Google Sheets spreadsheet and call it "My final project".
Step 2. In cell A1, write your candidate research question (the first one that comes to mind).
Step 3. Test your question against the three qualities: does it matter to you? can it be measured? is it concrete?
Step 4. If it fails on any of them, rewrite the question until it passes the three tests.
Step 5. Write the scope in A3: what it includes, what it leaves out, and the dates the data covers.
Step 6. Write the deliverable in A5: report, dashboard, presentation or poster.
Step 7. Make a schedule in the following rows: one row per stage of the cycle, with a start and an end date.
Step 8. Write in one cell which data you will need and where you will get it from.
Step 9. Write in another cell the ethical question: "is there other people's data in my project? How will I protect it?"
Step 10. Save the sheet. You have planted the seed of the Canopy: question, scope, deliverable and calendar, all in writing.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What are the three qualities of a good research question?
2. Why is "is public transport good?" not a good question?
3. What is the scope and why is it important to cut it?
4. What is a deliverable and why is it a good idea to decide it at the beginning?
5. What four elements should the plan of your final project have?

Answers: 1. That it matters to you, that it can be measured and that it is concrete. 2. Because "good" is an opinion, it cannot be counted or measured. 3. The boundary of the project (what is in and what is out); a small finished scope is worth more than a big abandoned one. 4. The final result (report, dashboard, presentation); deciding it at the beginning shapes all the work. 5. Question, scope, deliverable and schedule.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Your final project is your letter of introduction, and ethics will be your signature. Three commitments: first, honesty with the data, without polishing it so that it says what you want (Level 12: deceptive axes). Second, respect for people: if there is other people's data, consent and anonymity. Third, humility in your conclusions: if your data only shows a correlation (Level 10), say so and do not sell it as causation. A small, honest project is worth more than a big one decorated with lies.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- The project starts with the question, not with the data (think backwards).
- Good question: one that matters to you, is measurable and concrete.
- The scope is cut, not stretched.
- Plan with four elements: question, scope, deliverable and schedule.
