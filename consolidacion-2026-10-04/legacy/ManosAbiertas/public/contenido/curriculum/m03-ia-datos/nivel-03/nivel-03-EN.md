# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 03
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed

## 🎯 Level objective
- Learn what a spreadsheet is and what it is for in daily life.
- Understand what cells, rows and columns are.
- Enter data in a spreadsheet without being afraid of breaking anything.
- Know how to save and reopen a spreadsheet.
- Move the paper table from the previous levels onto the computer.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Spreadsheet | A notebook of rows and boxes on the screen, where data are written down. |
| Cell | Each individual box where one piece of data is written. |
| Row | The horizontal line of cells. It is named with numbers: row 1, row 2. |
| Column | The vertical line of cells. It is named with letters: A, B, C. |
| Sheet | Each working tab inside the file. One file can hold several sheets. |

## 📚 Main lesson
A spreadsheet is like a lined notebook, but much smarter. In a paper notebook, if you want to add up a hundred numbers, you have to do it with a calculator. In a spreadsheet, you type the numbers and ask the sheet to add them up. The sheet does the sum by itself and, if you change a number, the sum updates by itself. That is the magic we are going to discover today.

Think of a spreadsheet as a kitchen table with many cells. Each cell is a small box where one piece of data fits: a number, a word or a date. The cells are organised in rows and columns. Rows go from top to bottom and are named with numbers: row 1, row 2, row 3. Columns go from left to right and are named with letters: column A, column B, column C. Each cell has its own "address": the one in column B and row 3 is called B3. Like on a treasure-hunt board.

Why is the cell address important? Because formulas use it. In level 5 we will learn formulas, but it is worth understanding the idea now: if you tell the sheet "add up everything in column B", the sheet needs to know which column is B. Cell addresses are the language we speak to the spreadsheet. It is like knowing the names of the ingredients before following a recipe.

Let us start with the basics: opening a spreadsheet. The simplest and freest is Google Sheets, which opens in the browser, with nothing to install. There is also LibreOffice Calc, which you install on the computer and works offline. They work almost the same way. When you open Google Sheets, you will see a huge grid: cells, rows and columns everywhere. Do not panic: that grid is yours. You can write in it, delete, move and change whatever you want. There is no dangerous button that will break the computer.

To write in a cell, click on it with the mouse. You will see it get a border. Now you can type directly: what you type appears in the cell. To confirm, press the Enter key. The border jumps to the cell below, ready for the next entry. It is like writing line by line in a notebook, but in boxes.

And what if we make a mistake? Nothing is easier to fix. Click on the cell and type again, or press the Delete key to clear its contents. Another option: double-click the cell to edit just part of the text. Mistakes are not a problem in a spreadsheet: they are fixed in seconds. That is one of the great advantages over paper.

Another basic operation: adjusting the width of a column. If you type a long text and it does not fit, the word spills into the neighbouring cell. To fix it, move the mouse over the line that separates the column letters (between B and C, for example) and drag until there is enough space. It is like widening a pantry shelf so the box fits.

Saving your work is another key step. The good news: in Google Sheets you do not need to worry. The file saves itself, to your Google account, while you work. Every few seconds a copy is saved. It is like having an invisible helper who keeps the recipe safe while you cook. To give the file a name, click on the title at the top ("Untitled spreadsheet") and type whatever name you like.

In LibreOffice Calc, on the other hand, you must save by hand. Look for the floppy-disk icon (a small blue square) or the "File" and "Save" menu. Choose a folder, give it a clear name and confirm. Get used to saving every few minutes: it is the difference between losing half an hour of work or keeping it. As grandmothers say: "better safe than sorry".

Now, what can we already do with a spreadsheet? A great deal, even without formulas. We can type the expenses table we drew on paper, sort it with the A-Z button we saw in level 2, and fix mistakes on the spot. We can make the shopping budget, the family medication schedule or the Christmas gift list with prices. All of that is already working with data.

A hygiene tip: always use a header row with the column names (date, product, price...). That way, a year from now, when you open the file again, you will know what each column means. Also put each type of data in its own column: dates in one, prices in another. Never mix text and numbers in the same column if you can avoid it. That "pantry" order will save you headaches later.

Today's activity will be moving the paper table from level 1 into Google Sheets. That way you will see the difference between paper and screen: on paper, fixing a date meant crossing it out and writing over it; on screen, it is one click. On paper, adding up meant getting out the calculator; on screen, the sheet can do it for you (that will be level 5). Today we just write and save. Tomorrow, the sheet will start working.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: The shopping budget
Open a new sheet and write in the first row: "product", "quantity", "price". Below, write the 5 products you will buy this week. You now have your shopping list on the computer, sortable and correctable.

### Example 2: The medication schedule
On a sheet write the columns "medicine", "dose", "time", "who takes it". Write down the medicines your family takes. This sheet, well maintained, prevents dangerous forgetfulness.

### Example 3: The gift list
Create a sheet with "person", "gift", "price", "bought (yes/no)". Each gift is a row. You will be able to sort by person or see how many gifts you still have to buy.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open your browser and go to https://sheets.google.com.
Step 2. Sign in with your Google account. If you do not have one, ask for help creating one.
Step 3. Click the large "New" button or the "+" icon to create a blank sheet.
Step 4. Click on cell A1 and type "date". Press Enter.
Step 5. Type in B1 "product", in C1 "quantity" and in D1 "price". Press Enter after each one.
Step 6. Take the paper table from level 1 (your 5 purchases) and type each row: in A2 the date, in B2 the product, in C2 the quantity, in D2 the price.
Step 7. Click the title at the top ("Untitled spreadsheet") and type "My week's expenses". Press Enter.
Step 8. Type one more row: today's purchase with its real date and price.
Step 9. Look at the status bar: Google Sheets saves automatically. There is no save button.
Step 10. Close the browser tab. To get the sheet back, return to https://sheets.google.com and you will see your file in the list. Open it and check that everything is still there.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a cell and how is its address named?
2. What is the difference between a row and a column?
3. How do you write a piece of data in a cell and how do you confirm it?
4. What do you have to do to change the width of a column?
5. How do you save in Google Sheets and how do you get the file back later?

Answers: 1. It is each box where a piece of data is written; its address combines the column letter and the row number, for example B3. 2. The row is horizontal (named with numbers); the column is vertical (named with letters). 3. Click on the cell, type, and press Enter. 4. Drag with the mouse the line that separates the column letters. 5. It saves automatically to your Google account; to recover it, open https://sheets.google.com and click on the file.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
In a spreadsheet we keep data about our lives: purchases, medicines, relatives. If the file is on Google, protect it with your account and do not share it with strangers. Before sharing a sheet with someone, look at what it contains: a gift list can reveal more than it seems. And remember: health data (like the medication schedule) is among the most sensitive; care for it the way you would care for your doctor's prescription.
