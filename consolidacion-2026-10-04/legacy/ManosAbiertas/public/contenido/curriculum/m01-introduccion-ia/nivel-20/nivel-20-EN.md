# Module 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — Level 20
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Crown
## Estimated time: 3 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Think like a creator: start from a real problem, not from technology.
- Decide when AI is the right tool and when it is not.
- Define what data, what tool, and what test a solution needs.
- Set clear and simple success criteria.
- Design a small AI project from start to finish.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Problem | The real need you want to solve (a difficulty of a person or group). |
| Solution | The concrete way to solve it; it may use AI or not. |
| Requirements | What the solution must meet to serve: fast, cheap, simple, safe. |
| Prototype | A first simple version to test before investing effort. |
| Feasibility | Whether the solution is possible and reasonable with the available resources. |
| Success criterion | How to know the solution works: "the user understands it in 5 minutes". |

## 📚 Main lesson
So far we have learned to understand AI. This level takes a leap: we will learn to create solutions with it. But there is a rule many skip: first the problem, then the technology. Whoever starts with technology looks for answers to questions nobody asked.

Think like a cook who wants to open a restaurant. They do not start by buying knives: they start by thinking about what food they want to offer and to whom. The same applies to AI. Before asking "what AI do I use?", ask: "what problem do I want to solve?". The tool comes later.

The first step is to describe the problem in simple words. Not "I want an app with AI", but "my 78-year-old mother forgets to take her afternoon medication". The more concrete the problem, the easier the solution. Vague problems produce vague solutions.

The second step is the most valuable and the most forgotten: deciding whether AI is needed. Not all problems are AI problems. A medication reminder can simply be an alarm on the phone. Sometimes paper and a pen are the best technology. Using AI because it is pretty is like buying a tractor for a small vegetable patch.

If AI seems useful, the third step is to ask what it needs to learn. All AI feeds on data. What data do we have? Is it enough? Is it good quality? If there is no data, there is no learning, and maybe the solution is simpler than it looks.

The fourth step is choosing the tool. Here the ladder rule applies: first the simplest thing that works. A reminder can be an alarm; a list can be a spreadsheet; a translation can be a free translator. Only if the simple thing is not enough do we go up a step.

The fifth step is to think about the prototype: a small, fast version to test. It does not need to be perfect. A prototype is like trying the recipe at home before cooking for forty guests. Testing early and cheaply avoids big failures.

The sixth step is to define the success criterion. How will we know it works? A good criterion is simple and measurable: "my mother remembers to take her pill 6 out of 7 days", "the member finds the document in less than a minute". Without a criterion, there is no way to know if the solution helped.

The seventh step, and perhaps the most human, is to think about the people: who will use the solution, who could be harmed, who could be left out. A solution that excludes older people or that scares users is a failed solution, even if the technique is perfect.

Let us see a complete example. The neighborhood association wants the meeting minutes to be summarized. The problem: meetings last two hours and nobody wants to read them. The question: is AI needed? For summarizing, yes: an assistant can summarize an audio in ten lines. The data: the meeting audio. The tool: a free transcription and a text assistant. The prototype: summarize the next meeting and email it. The success: 80% of members read it.

And the cost? AI can be free or cheap, but it is never without cost: it consumes energy, time, and attention. You must ask whether the benefit justifies the expense. Sometimes a ten-minute manual solution is better than a month of programming.

You also have to think about what can go wrong. The summary can be wrong. The alarm can fail. The assistant can invent data. Every AI solution needs a plan B: a person who reviews, a duplicate alert, a reliable source. Trust is built with safeguards.

This level does not ask you to build anything yet: it asks you to design. And designing is a skill you train. Every time you identify a problem, describe it clearly, ask yourself whether AI is needed, think about the data, choose the simple thing, test small, and define success, you are designing like a professional.

In the next level, we will continue in the Crown with an essential skill for the AI era: critically evaluating news about artificial intelligence.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Medication reminder:** the problem is memory, not technology; the solution can be a simple alarm.
2. **Meeting summaries:** the association uses free transcription plus a text assistant to summarize its meetings.
3. **Shop inventory:** the merchant records sales in a spreadsheet before considering an AI; they only level up if the simple thing is not enough.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Choose a real problem from your life or your environment (home, association, work). Write: "My problem is that...".
Step 2. Describe it in one concrete sentence, with the people affected. Example: "my mother forgets her afternoon medication".
Step 3. Ask yourself: is AI needed or is a simple solution enough? Write down your answer with a reason.
Step 4. If AI is useful, what data would it need? Write them down.
Step 5. Choose the simplest tool that could work (alarm, spreadsheet, free assistant).
Step 6. Define a small prototype: what will you test this week?
Step 7. Define the success criterion with a number: "6 out of 7 days", "5 minutes", "80%".
Step 8. Write the plan B: what will you do if the solution fails? And share the design with someone to improve it.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Why is it better to start with the problem and not with technology?
2. When is it NOT necessary to use AI?
3. What is a prototype and why is it useful?
4. What is a success criterion and why must it be measurable?
5. What is the plan B and why does every AI solution need one?

**Answers:** 1) Because technology without a problem is an answer to a question nobody asked; starting from the problem guarantees the solution serves. 2) When a simple solution (alarm, paper, spreadsheet) already solves the problem. 3) It is a first small version to test before investing effort; it avoids large and cheap failures. 4) It is the way to know if the solution works; it must be measurable to be able to verify success. 5) It is the safeguard against AI failures: a person who reviews, a duplicate alert, or a reliable source.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Designing solutions with AI is designing power, and power demands responsibility. A solution can exclude those who do not use technology, waste extra energy, or make decisions that harm. Before building, ask: who benefits? Who is left out? What happens if it is wrong? A good design does not only work: it is fair, understandable, and reviewable by people.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- First the problem, then the technology.
- Not all problems need AI.
- Data, simple tool, and small prototype.
- Success is defined with numbers, not opinions.
- Every solution needs a plan B and a fair distribution.
