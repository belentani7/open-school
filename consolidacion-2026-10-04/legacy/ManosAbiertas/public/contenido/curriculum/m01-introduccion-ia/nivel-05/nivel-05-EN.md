## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root

## 🎯 Level objective
- Learn the main stages of the history of AI, from Turing to ChatGPT.
- Understand the Turing test and why it is famous.

- Understand how we went from a chess program to an assistant that converses.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Turing test | A test invented by Alan Turing: if we cannot tell whether we are talking to a machine or a person, the machine "thinks". |
| Computation | The ability of a device to make calculations and process information using rules. |
| AI winter | Periods in history when AI failed to keep its promises and interest and funding were lost. |
| Neural network | A system inspired by the brain that learns from examples; the basis of modern AI. |
| Language model | A program trained on a huge amount of text to understand and generate human language. |
| Milestone | A moment or achievement that marks a before and an after. |

## 📚 Main lesson
The history of artificial intelligence is older than it seems, and it did not start with phones. It begins with a question humanity has asked for centuries: can a machine think? The first important name is Alan Turing, a British mathematician who, during World War II, built a machine capable of deciphering secret messages and thus saved millions of lives.

In 1950, Turing published a paper with a famous question: "Can machines think?" Since "thinking" was hard to measure, he proposed a practical test, which today we call the Turing test: if a person converses with a machine and with another person without knowing which is which, and cannot tell them apart, then the machine has passed the test. It is a simple and powerful idea: judge intelligence by behavior, not by theory.

Shortly after, in 1956, a group of scientists gathered at Dartmouth College in the United States and coined the term "artificial intelligence". In those years, optimism was enormous: it was believed that within a few decades machines would reason like people. Programs were built that played checkers, solved logic problems, and translated simple sentences from Russian.

But disappointment arrived. The problems that are easy for people (seeing, walking, understanding a language) turned out to be extremely hard for machines. The promises were not kept, and in the seventies and eighties the so-called "AI winters" occurred: investments fell, laboratories closed, and people lost interest. The history of AI is also the history of its disappointments.

Even so, in the eighties "expert systems" appeared: programs with rules written by hand by specialists that helped diagnose diseases or explore oil wells. They worked, but they were rigid: they only knew what had been hand-taught to them. They did not learn by themselves. The key to the future was learning, not rules.

The big change came when machines began to learn from data instead of following written rules. That is machine learning: the program trains with examples and finds patterns by itself. Faster and faster computers and more and more abundant data made the impossible possible.

In 1997, IBM's Deep Blue machine beat the world chess champion, Garry Kasparov. It was a huge milestone: the press all over the world told the story. But Deep Blue won by brute force: it calculated millions of moves per second. It did not "understand" chess the way Kasparov did. Even so, the world understood that machines could beat humans at specific tasks.

The next leap came with deep learning, neural networks with many layers. In 2012, a system of this kind won an image recognition contest called ImageNet in a decisive way. Suddenly, machines "saw" better than ever. From then on, everything accelerated: speech recognition, translation, self-driving cars, medicine.

In 2016, another machine, AlphaGo, beat the world champion of Go, an Eastern game far more complex than chess. The surprising thing is that AlphaGo made moves that experts considered mistakes and that later turned out to be brilliant. The machine had learned things no human had discovered. That impressed millions of people.

And in November 2022, ChatGPT arrived. A conversation assistant that anyone could use by typing in a browser, without installing anything, for free. In five days it reached one million users, an absolute record. Suddenly, AI stopped being a laboratory thing and moved into everyone's pocket. The Turing test, in a way, had come very close.

ChatGPT does not "think" like a person. It is a language model: it learned to predict words by reading an immense amount of text from the internet. That is why it converses fluently, but it is also why it sometimes makes things up. It is like a student who has memorized many books and answers with what it has read, even though it sometimes confuses reality with what it read.

The lesson of history is twofold. First, AI is the fruit of decades of work, of triumphs and failures. Second, big leaps arrive when the way of teaching machines changes: from rules to data. And we live in a unique moment: the biggest leap since the discipline exists.

Understanding history gives us perspective. What today seems like magic is the result of many small steps. And what comes next will be another step, built on this one. History has not ended: it is being written now, and you are already part of it.

In the next level, we will see the types of AI: narrow, general, and superintelligent, and why most of what we use today is "narrow".

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **At the bank:** today's fraud detection systems are heirs of the "expert systems" of the 1980s.
2. **At home:** the voice assistant you use every morning is a fruit of the deep learning boom of 2012.
3. **At the doctor's office:** systems that help read X-rays descend from ImageNet's neural networks.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What did Alan Turing propose in 1950 and what does his famous test measure?
2. What were the "AI winters" and why did they come?
3. How does Deep Blue (1997) differ from systems that learn from data?
4. What changed in 2012 with deep neural networks?
5. What is ChatGPT and why does it sometimes make up answers?

**Answers:** 1) He proposed that machines can think, and his test measures whether a person can distinguish the machine from a person while conversing. 2) Periods when AI failed to keep its promises and investments and interest fell. 3) Deep Blue won by calculating millions of moves, without learning; today's systems learn patterns from data. 4) Deep networks showed that machines could "see" and identify images better than ever, which accelerated all of AI. 5) It is a language model trained on huge amounts of text; it sometimes invents because it predicts plausible words, not because it verifies the truth.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
History teaches that technology reflects its time. Turing, who saved millions of lives, was persecuted for his condition and died unjustly. AI is not foreign to the values of those who build it. Today, those who design systems decide which data is used, which questions are answered, and what is true. That is why knowing history is also demanding that the next chapters be written with justice and with people at the center.
