# Module 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — Level 21
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Crown
## Estimated time: 3 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Read AI news with a critical eye, not with the gut.
- Spot exaggerated and sensationalist headlines.
- Verify the source, the date, and the origin of a news story.
- Recognize "AI washing": selling something as AI when it is not.
- Apply a checklist before sharing or believing a news story.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Sensationalism | Exaggerating a news story to get attention, at the cost of truth. |
| Primary source | The original origin of the information: the study, the report, the direct statement. |
| Headline | The big phrase that summarizes the news; many times it hides what the text says. |
| Clickbait | A trap headline designed to make us click, with promises it does not keep. |
| AI washing | Calling "AI" something that is a normal tool, to seem modern. |
| Verification | Checking data, dates, and sources before treating a news story as true. |

## 📚 Main lesson
AI is on every front page. "AI will end your job", "a medical robot saves a patient", "AI discovers the cure for everything". Some stories are true, others exaggerated, and others directly false. Knowing how to tell them apart is as important a skill as knowing how to use your phone.

Think about how you evaluate a rumor in the neighborhood. If someone tells you the supermarket is closing, you do not run out: you ask who said it, whether they saw it, when. With AI news you have to do the same, but more carefully, because the noise is enormous and very fast.

The first question is: where does the story come from? A serious outlet, with a newsroom and journalists, is not the same as a page that copy-pastes headlines to win clicks. Look at the outlet's name, its track record, whether it has an author, and whether it cites sources. News without an author is already a warning sign.

The second question is: when was it published? In technology, everything ages fast. A three-year-old story about an AI achievement can be completely outdated. Sharing an old story as if it were today's is an involuntary form of misinformation.

The third question is the most important: what is the primary source? Behind every serious AI story there is a study, a report, or a statement. When the headline shouts "AI cures", the original study surely says something more nuanced. Going to the primary source is like talking to the witness instead of the neighbor who heard the neighbor.

The fourth question is linguistic: what words does the headline use? "Will destroy", "finally", "impossible", "revolutionary" are words of emotion, not of information. A serious headline is usually boring: "researchers present a model that improves a task by 3%". Boredom, in science, is usually a good sign.

The fifth question is technical: does AI really play a part? "AI washing" consists of calling anything AI to seem modern. An app that does sums with formulas is not AI. A standard email filter is not AI. When the headline says "with AI", check whether there really is machine learning or just marketing.

The sixth question is about interests: who benefits from the story? A company launching a product benefits from fear or enthusiasm. A politician benefits from us believing that robots take our jobs. Every story has someone behind it who gains something from us believing it.

The seventh question is the one about evidence: what proof does the story show? Are there data, names, links to the study, real images? If there are only opinions and promises, it is opinion, not news. And remember what we learned in level 18: images can be generated. Even the news photo can be fake.

It is also worth distinguishing between future and present. Many headlines talk about what AI "will be able to do", not what it does. "AI will be able to diagnose" is not "AI diagnoses". The future is the future: interesting to dream about, but not to decide anyone's life.

And what do you do with all this? First, do not share a headline without reading the full article: the headline is a door, not the house. Second, if the story seems too good or too bad to be true, it is almost certainly exaggerated. Third, use the checklist: source, date, primary source, words, real AI, interests, evidence.

Being critical is not being negative. It is not about distrusting everything, but about giving each story its fair value. AI does truly astonishing things, and reading them well lets us enjoy them without falling into fear or fantasy. The truth is usually in the middle ground.

For people over 40, this skill is a precious defense. In an age of shouting headlines, knowing how to read in silence is an advantage. You do not need to be an expert: you need to ask seven questions before believing.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Trap headline:** "AI will destroy half of all jobs" vs. the original report that says "some tasks will change in specific sectors within a decade".
2. **AI washing:** a company sells as "AI" a simple spreadsheet with formulas to seem modern.
3. **Fake photo:** a news story uses an AI-generated image as if it were a real photo of an event.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Choose an AI news story you have seen this week (on TV, WhatsApp, or the Internet).
Step 2. Write down the exact headline and the outlet that published it.
Step 3. Question 1 (source): is it a serious outlet? Does it have an author?
Step 4. Question 2 (date): when was it published? Is it outdated?
Step 5. Question 3 (primary source): search Google for the original study or report. What does it really say?
Step 6. Questions 4 and 5 (words and real AI): are there emotional words? Does AI really play a part?
Step 7. Questions 6 and 7 (interests and evidence): who benefits? What proof does it show?
Step 8. Write your verdict in one sentence: is it news, exaggeration, or rumor? And decide whether you would share it.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Why are boring headlines usually a good sign in science?
2. What is the primary source and why should you look for it?
3. What is "AI washing"?
4. What is the difference between "AI will be able to diagnose" and "AI diagnoses"?
5. Write the seven-question checklist.

**Answers:** 1) Because emotion and exaggeration usually go in the flashy headlines; what is serious is usually measured with numbers and nuances. 2) It is the original origin of the information (study, report, statement); you should look for it because headlines simplify it. 3) It is calling a normal tool "AI" just to seem modern, without real machine learning. 4) The first talks about the future, a promise; the second about the present, a fact; they are not the same and must be read differently. 5) Source, date, primary source, emotional words, real AI, interests, and evidence.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Sharing a false or exaggerated news story is also misinformation, even if it is not intentional. You have the power to cut the chain: do not share what is not verified, warn respectfully when someone spreads something doubtful, and do not feed fear. The responsibility for information is collective, and every click and every forward counts.

## 🔓 Open tools
- **Google Scholar** (scholar.google.com): search the original studies behind the news.
- **Google News** (news.google.com): compare how different outlets tell the same story.
- **Snopes** (snopes.com): rumor and fake news checker.
- **Reuters Fact Check** (reuters.com/fact-check): fact-checking of AI and technology news.
- **Wikipedia** (wikipedia.org): summaries with references to cross-check AI topics.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- Headlines shout; the truth whispers.
- Seven questions before believing: source, date, origin, words, real AI, interests, evidence.
- Going to the primary source is the best defense.
- AI washing sells smoke.
- Do not share what you do not verify: you cut the chain.
