# Module 7: Community Projects — Level 09
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand why it is good for the project to grow with new people.
- Learn to welcome newcomers well, with welcome and patience.
- Explain the project clearly to those who do not know it.
- Share out the work also with those who arrive now.
- Make sure no newcomer feels left out.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Grow | For the project to gain more people and more strength. |
| Welcome | The way of receiving newcomers well, with joy and attention. |
| New participant | The person who joins the project now, with their energy. |
| Mentor | Someone who accompanies and guides a new participant in their first steps. |
| Integration | The process of making newcomers feel part of the group. |
| Welcoming | The attitude of giving space, time and affection to newcomers. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Every living project grows. Like a plant that gains new leaves, a project that works attracts people. A neighbour hears about the garden and wants to help; a friend sees the festival and asks if she can take part. Growing is a good sign: it means the project is worthwhile and the community feels it as its own.

Growing brings clear advantages. More people, more hands and more ideas. There is more energy for the tasks, more joy in the gatherings and more voices for thinking. A small project tires the same people always; a project that grows shares out the work and renews its strength.

But growing also demands care. Welcoming new people is not only opening the door: it is preparing the house. Newcomers do not know the objective, nor the plan, nor everyone's names. If nobody explains anything, they feel lost and may leave. The project must be good at welcoming, not only at inviting.

The first rule of welcoming is the personal touch. When someone new appears, someone from the group receives them by name, introduces them to the others and shows them the space. You do not leave the newcomer alone in a corner, not knowing who to talk to. A warm reception, like at home, is worth more than a thousand posters.

The second rule is to explain the project clearly. Newcomers need to know three things: what we do, why we do it and what they can do. No speech is needed: a simple conversation, calmly and without hurry, is enough. "This project is a community garden. We want the neighbourhood to have fresh vegetables and a place to meet. You can help us on Saturdays."

The third rule is to give a first small task. The new participant will not coordinate anything at first: they will start with something simple and concrete, where they can help and learn. A small, well-supervised task makes them feel useful from the first day. Confidence grows by doing, not by watching.

The fourth rule is the figure of the mentor. It is good for each new person to have someone who accompanies them in the first weeks: someone who teaches them, answers their questions and introduces them to the rest. The mentor is not a boss: they are an experienced friend who lends a hand in the first steps. Like the grandmother who teaches her grandson to make bread.

The mentor is chosen among the most experienced participants, and is usually the one with the most patience, not the one in the most hurry. Over time, the new participant stops needing support and can themselves become a mentor for another person. That way the welcoming passes from hand to hand, like a family recipe.

The fifth rule is to be patient with everyone's pace. Not everyone learns at the same pace, nor can everyone do the same. A new participant may need explanations twice, and that is normal. The group that laughs at those who ask clips the wings of newcomers; the group that thanks every question builds trust.

It is also important to take care of those who arrive with less ease: those with little time, those who speak another language or those who feel shy. The welcome is the same for everyone, but with more attention to those who need it most. Tasks can be adapted: there is always a place for a person who can only give one hour a week.

Growing also forces you to share out. When new people arrive, the coordinator presents the plan and the tasks that need people. They ask: what would you like to do? Newcomers choose with pleasure what suits them, and the work is shared out better. The new participant is not an extra hand: they are a person with tastes and abilities.

Sometimes big teams are frightening. But there is no need to fear: a large group works when there are clear roles and a good welcome. The team is not measured by its number, but by how it takes care of each person. A team that welcomes well attracts; a team that ignores pushes away, even if it is many.

In this level we will prepare the welcome of our project. We will write a simple presentation, think about the welcome task and choose who will be the mentor of newcomers. We will make our team an open door, not a closed one.

Let us remember: everyone who belongs to the project today was once new. Those who were well received, receive well. The welcome is a debt of gratitude that we pay to every new person who arrives, like those who receive at home what was once received.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The neighbour who asks.** A neighbour sees the garden and asks how it works. The coordinator receives her, introduces her and explains the project in two sentences. He invites her to a Saturday session. She comes, does a small task with her mentor and, three months later, coordinates the compost.
2. **The group that welcomes in Spanish and English.** At the association there are people who speak English and people who speak Spanish. When someone who only speaks Spanish arrives, the team assigns a bilingual mentor and the posters are made in both languages. Nobody is left out because of language.
3. **The shy young woman.** A shy young woman appears at the festival and stays in a corner. The person responsible goes over, talks with her and gives her a simple task: looking after the entrance. At the end, everyone thanks her by name. At the next meeting, she already speaks and proposes ideas.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Write a presentation of your project in three sentences: what it is, why it exists and how to take part.
Step 2: Think about the welcome task: someone from the group always receives newcomers, by name and with a smile.
Step 3: Prepare a list of small tasks for new participants, simple and supervised.
Step 4: Choose one or two mentors among the most experienced participants, who have patience and enjoy teaching.
Step 5: Plan the first session of a new participant: introduction, conversation, small task and thanks.
Step 6: Think about how to adapt the tasks for those with less time or less ease.
Step 7: Review the welcome with the team: are we receiving well? Is there anyone who arrived and was left out?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What does it mean for a project to grow? a) That it gains problems. b) That it gains more people and more strength. c) That it has to change its name.
2. What do we do first when someone new appears? a) Receive them by name and introduce them. b) Give them a speech about the rules. c) Let them watch from the corner.
3. What must newcomers know? a) The life of each participant. b) What we do, why we do it and what they can do. c) The whole history of the neighbourhood.
4. What is the mentor for? a) To give orders to the new participant. b) To accompany and guide them in their first steps. c) To do the new person's work.
5. What task do we give a new participant first? a) The hardest one, to test them. b) The one of coordinating everything. c) A small, simple and supervised task.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-c.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Welcoming newcomers is an obligation of the project, not a favour.
- The new participant is a person, not just a working hand.
- Language, time or shyness cannot be barriers to taking part.
- The mentor accompanies without humiliating, guides without giving orders.
- The project grows to serve the community better, not to have a bigger name.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Growing is good when you welcome well: receiving by name, explaining clearly, giving a small task and putting a mentor beside newcomers. The welcome turns a group into a home. In the next level we will learn to speak with the community: presenting the project in the square, in the shops and at neighbourhood meetings, and using simple publicity to reach more people.
