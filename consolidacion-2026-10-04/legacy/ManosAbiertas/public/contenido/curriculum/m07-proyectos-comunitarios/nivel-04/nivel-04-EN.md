# Module 7: Community Projects — Level 04
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand why a project needs clear objectives.
- Learn to write simple objectives: specific, achievable and with a date.
- Tell a good objective apart from a vague one.
- Write the first objectives of our own project.
- Know how to review the objectives and adjust them when necessary.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Objective | The concrete goal we want to reach, written clearly. |
| Specific | An objective that says exactly what is to be achieved, without beating around the bush. |
| Achievable | An objective that can really be reached with our resources. |
| Deadline | The day by which we expect to have achieved the objective. |
| Result | What is seen when the objective is met. |
| Review | Looking at the objectives again to see if we are on track or need to adjust them. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous levels we learned to communicate with the team. Now comes the most important part of project thinking: defining where we are heading. Because a team without a clear objective is like a boat without a course: it moves, but you do not know if it is advancing. An objective is the star that guides the boat.

Let us think of the recipe metaphor. If we want to cook lentils, the recipe says exactly which ingredients and which steps to follow. An objective is the part of the recipe that states the result: "today we eat lentils with vegetables". If we only said "today we make something to eat", we would not know what to buy or how much time to spend.

A good objective must have three qualities. The first is that it is specific: it says exactly what we want, without vague words. Saying "we want the neighbourhood to be better" (vague) is not the same as "we want to fix the three broken benches in the square" (specific).

The second quality is that it is achievable. That means it can be reached with what we have: people, time and resources. Dreaming is good, but objectives are written with the possible in mind. Better a small objective achieved than a huge one failed.

The third quality is that it has a date. A deadline turns a wish into a commitment. "We want to fix the benches" cannot be measured. "We want to fix the benches before the June festival" can: we will know whether we achieved it or not.

Writing objectives forces us to choose. A project cannot achieve ten things at once, just as we cannot eat ten dishes in a single meal. Choosing the main objective is deciding what matters most to the community and focusing our efforts there.

It is also important that objectives are understood. If we write them in a complicated way, the team will not know what to do. An objective is written in a simple sentence that any person in the group can repeat. If it cannot be explained in one sentence, it is not well written yet.

Let us see the difference with a garden example. Vague objective: "to have a pretty garden". Well-written objective: "to plant tomatoes, lettuces and carrots in the plot on Main Street and harvest the first crop before September". Now we know what to do, with whom and when.

Objectives can be divided into small steps. Each step is also an objective, but shorter: "buy the seeds this week", "prepare the soil on Saturday", "sow at the weekend". It is like slicing the ham: easier to chew.

As the project advances, objectives are reviewed. Sometimes we discover they were too ambitious for the time we have, and we adjust them. Other times we see we underestimated what the team can do. Reviewing is not giving up: it is being sensible and honest.

Objectives must be written down. It is not enough to keep them in your head: on paper, all the members see them and remember them. They can be written in a notebook, in a pinned note on the WhatsApp group, or on a poster on the wall of the common room.

In a community project, the objective is not decided by one person: it is decided by the team together with the community. Before fixing the goal, it is wise to ask the neighbours what they truly need. An objective chosen by everyone has much more strength to be fulfilled.

To finish, let us remember the relationship between objective and result. The objective is the promise; the result is what we see at the end. When the objective is well written, comparing the result is easy: were the benches fixed? Was there a harvest in September? Yes or no, and that way we will know if the project worked.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The reading club.** The team wants to encourage reading among older people. Instead of saying "we want people to read more", they write: "to create a reading club at the library, with a monthly meeting, starting in March and with at least ten participants in the first quarter".
2. **The park clean-up.** The residents of a neighbourhood write their objective: "to clean the park on the corner and repair the broken swing before Children's Day". With that objective, they know how many bags to buy and when to call the team together.
3. **The phone classes.** An association wants to teach older people how to use their phones. Its objective is concrete: "to give a two-hour workshop a week at the community centre, from October to December, so that twenty people learn to make video calls to their families".

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take the paper of your project idea (level 00).
Step 2: Write a sentence that begins like this: "Our objective is..."
Step 3: Check whether it is specific: ask yourself if someone outside the team would understand exactly what you want to do. If not, add more details (where, what, for whom).
Step 4: Check whether it is achievable: do you have the people, the time and the means to achieve it? If not, reduce the objective to something smaller.
Step 5: Add the date: write "before..." or "by the month of...". The date turns the wish into a commitment.
Step 6: Divide the objective into three small steps, each with its own short date.
Step 7: Read the objective out loud to a person on your team and ask whether they understand it. If they understand it at first reading, the objective is well written.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What are objectives for in a project? a) To show off to the neighbours. b) To know where we are going and whether we achieved it. c) To make the team's life complicated.
2. Which of these is a specific objective? a) "We want the neighbourhood to be better". b) "We want to fix the three broken benches in the square". c) "We want to do nice things".
3. What is missing from an objective with no date? a) Nothing, it is perfect. b) The commitment of knowing when it is met. c) More words.
4. Who decides the objective of a community project? a) Only the coordinator. b) The team, taking into account the community's opinion. c) The town hall, always.
5. If the objective turns out to be too big, what do we do? a) Give up on the project. b) Review it and adjust it to something achievable. c) Carry on the same even if it is not met.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- The objectives of a community project must be born from the real needs of the community, not from one person's ideas.
- Writing clear objectives is also a matter of honesty: we only promise what we can truly deliver.
- Let us not use objectives to seek personal limelight or recognition. The success belongs to the team and the community.
- Adjusting an objective is not failure; it is responsibility. Better a realistic objective than an unkept promise.
- Objectives must be inclusive: let us also think of people with fewer resources, less mobility or less access to technology.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
A clear objective says exactly what we want, whether it can be achieved and by when. It is written in a simple sentence, divided into small steps and reviewed honestly. The objective guides the whole team like the recipe guides the cook. With communication learned and objectives clear, we finish the Seed band. In the next band, Root, we will learn to plan the work and share out the tasks among the team members.
