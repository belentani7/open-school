# Module 7: Community Projects — Level 05
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what planning is and why it saves time and trouble.
- Learn to divide a project into stages and small tasks.
- Put the tasks in order over time with a simple calendar.
- Identify what each task needs: people and materials.
- Create the first plan of our project, simple and realistic.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Plan | Thinking before acting: deciding which steps we will take and in what order. |
| Stage | A big part of the project, like planting, caring for or harvesting. |
| Task | A small, concrete action, like buying seeds. |
| Calendar | The sheet where we mark when each task is done. |
| Deadline | The maximum time to finish a task or a stage. |
| Unexpected event | Something that was not planned for and has to be dealt with, like an unexpected rain. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We already know where we are heading: we have a clear objective with a date. Now it is the road's turn: how to get there. Planning is drawing that road before starting to walk. It seems like an extra step, but it saves time, money and trouble. Those who do not plan end up doing things roughly, like someone cooking without looking at the recipe and then being surprised that the salt is missing.

Let us imagine we want to travel to another city. Nobody takes the car without knowing which road to take. First we look at the map, calculate the hours, decide where to sleep and what to bring. That is planning. The community project works the same way: first you think, then you act.

Planning has a first rule: divide the project into stages. If our objective is to have a community garden, the stages could be preparing the soil, sowing, caring and harvesting. Each stage is a big, orderly part of the road.

Each stage is divided into small tasks. Preparing the soil becomes: cleaning the plot, asking for permission, buying tools, fertilizing the soil. A small task is easier to do than a whole stage: just as we do not eat the cake in one bite, but in slices.

The second rule is to put the tasks in order. Some things depend on others: you do not sow before preparing the soil, nor paint before cleaning. The natural order of the project is discovered by asking: what is needed before this?

The third rule is to set dates. You mark on the calendar when each task is done. Some tasks can be done at any time; others have a precise moment, like sowing tomatoes in spring. The calendar turns the plan into something real and visible.

The calendar can be on paper or on the phone. Many projects use a shared calendar that the whole team sees. But to start with, a sheet where the dates and tasks are written is enough. What matters is not the tool, but the habit of looking at it.

The fourth rule is to think about what we need for each task. Each task has its ingredients: people, materials and time. "Painting the fence" needs two people, a can of paint and a morning. If we think about the ingredients in advance, the day will not arrive without paint or without hands.

This is where realism comes in. Planning is not dreaming: it is calculating with what we have. If the team has four people and two hours a week, the plan must fit into that. An impossible plan discourages; a realistic plan is fulfilled and brings joy.

You also have to leave room for the unexpected. In projects, as in the garden, it rains when it is not supposed to. It is wise to allow a margin of time and not to push the dates to the limit. A day of margin between tasks is like the gap the seamstress leaves in the fabric before the hem.

Unexpected events are not an excuse: they are part of the project. When they arise, the team meets, adjusts the calendar and carries on. The difference between a project that advances and one that sinks is not the absence of problems, but the habit of solving them together.

A very simple planning tool is the list of tasks in the WhatsApp group. A message with the list and the dates can be pinned. When a task is finished, the person who did it writes "done". That way the team watches the plan advance, like watching a cake rise in the oven.

In this level we will learn to make our first plan. It does not have to be perfect: plans are adjusted as you go. What it must be is clear: everyone must know what is to be done and by when.

Let us remember the house metaphor. We do not build a house without blueprints. The project's blueprint is its plan: stages, tasks, dates and resources. With the blueprint in hand, the team works calmly, because it knows where it is and where it is going.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The festival plan.** The festival team divides its project into stages: permits, materials, food and music. Each stage has tasks with dates: applying for the licence before March, buying the lights in April, booking the band in May. Nobody improvises the day before.
2. **The community garden.** The garden plan marks: asking the town hall for permission in January, cleaning the plot in February, preparing the soil in March and sowing in April. When it rains and things are delayed a week, they adjust the calendar and carry on.
3. **The clothing drive.** An association wants to collect clothes for winter. Its plan: informing the neighbours in October, putting boxes in the shops in November and distributing the clothes in December. Each task has a person in charge and a date, and everything is reviewed in the group.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your objective written in level 04.
Step 2: Divide the objective into three stages. Write each stage on one line.
Step 3: Under each stage, write two or three small tasks. Ask yourself: what needs to be done to complete this stage?
Step 4: Put the tasks in order: number them according to the natural order (what goes before what).
Step 5: Next to each task, write an approximate date: "week of the 10th", "end of the month", etc. Use a paper calendar if it helps.
Step 6: Next to each task, write what is needed: people, materials and time.
Step 7: Review the whole plan with your team in the group or at a meeting. If something seems impossible, cut or move dates. A realistic plan is fulfilled better.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is planning for? a) To waste time. b) To save time, money and trouble. c) So the coordinator gives the orders.
2. What is a task? a) A big part of the project. b) A small, concrete action. c) An unexpected event.
3. What do we do if a task needs something we do not have? a) We leave it for the day and are surprised. b) We write it down in the plan and get it before the date. c) We change objectives.
4. What is an unexpected event? a) Something that was in the plan. b) Something that was not planned for and has to be dealt with. c) A team mistake.
5. What should a good plan be? a) Ambitious even if impossible. b) Clear, orderly and realistic. c) Long and complicated.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- A good plan respects the time of every person on the team: nobody should carry more work than another.
- Deadlines must be agreed by everyone, not imposed by a single person.
- If someone cannot do their part, they are helped and the plan is readjusted, without humiliation.
- The plan should also provide for people with fewer resources: zero-cost or very low-cost tasks whenever possible.
- Transparency in the plan (who does what and when) avoids rumours and misunderstandings in the community.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Planning is thinking the road before walking: dividing the project into stages, each stage into small tasks, putting them in order and giving them dates and resources. A realistic plan, with margin for the unexpected, is the foundation of a calm project. In the next level we will learn to share out those tasks among the people: the roles in a team and why each one is important.
