# Module 4: Content Generation — Level 04
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand that creations (photos, texts, songs) belong to someone.
- Know what copyright is, with a simple explanation.
- Know what can be used freely and what requires permission.
- Respect other people's work when we share or reuse content.
- Know that what we create with AI also raises questions of authorship.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Copyright | The right of the person who creates something to decide how their work is used. It is like being the owner of a recipe. |
| Author | The person who creates the work: the photographer, the writer, the musician. |
| Work | The result of creation: a photo, a text, a song, a video. |
| Free use | Being able to use a work without asking permission (for example, for personal use). |
| Cite | Mention who is the author of a work you use. Like saying "this was done by Maria". |
| Licence | The permission the author gives to use their work, with some conditions. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Imagine you have knitted a blanket with great care. It is yours: you decide who uses it, how and when. If someone takes it without permission, you get angry. The same happens with creations: photos, texts, songs and videos have an owner. That right of the creator is called "copyright".

Copyright is not a strange lawyer thing. It is common sense: whoever works has a right to their work. If a neighbour takes beautiful photos of the village, those photos are hers. If someone uses them in a magazine without asking her permission, they are violating her right.

Now, what about the photos we find on the internet? The golden rule is simple: if it is not clear that you can use it, it is better not to use it as if it were yours. Sharing for personal purposes, for example sending a photo to a relative, is usually acceptable. But publishing it on a website or using it to sell already requires permission.

There is an important detail: "not for profit" does not mean "no permission". Even if we do not make money from something, the work still has an owner. It is like the knitted blanket: the fact that we do not sell it does not mean anyone can take it.

How do we know if we can use an image or a text? We look for the licence. Many pages state whether the work is free to use or what conditions it has. Some works are "public domain": the author died long ago and everyone can now use them. Others have "Creative Commons" licences, which allow use under conditions, such as citing the author.

Citing the author is the simplest show of respect: writing under a photo "Photograph: Maria Garcia" or under a text the name of whoever wrote it. It costs nothing and avoids problems. It is like signing the blanket: if someone uses it, they know whose it is.

When AI creates content, the question becomes complicated. Who is the author of a generated image: the person who wrote the description, the company that made the program, or nobody? Each country has its own rules. In many places, works created by AI may be free of rights or have special authorship. That is why it is wise to check the terms of each tool.

The most practical thing for our level: if we generate an image or a text with AI for personal use, there is no problem. If we publish it or use it in a business, we read the terms and, if in doubt, we ask. Doubt is a good sign: it means we are being responsible.

We must also look after other people's content inside our creations. If we put a background song in a video, that song has an author. If we use a person's photo to illustrate something, that person has rights over their image. Respect extends to everything.

And let us not forget the opposite direction: we are also authors. The photos we take, the texts we write (even with the help of AI) and the videos we record belong to us. We can decide how they are used, and nobody should publish them without our permission.

Storing our things well is also part of authorship: keeping photos in the gallery, saving texts in a document, and not deleting the author's name when we share. Small habits that protect big rights.

The idea that must remain from this level is: behind every work there is a person. Using other people's content with respect and permission is not bureaucracy, it is living together. Like in the neighbourhood: we ask for the salt when we need it and we thank whoever gives it to us.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The neighbour's photo.** Maria finds a beautiful photo of her village taken by a neighbour. Instead of copying it, she asks her permission by WhatsApp. The neighbour says yes and is delighted.
2. **The blog text.** Pedro wants to put an article in the club newsletter. He asks the author whether he can use it, cites his name and everyone is happy.
3. **The AI-generated image.** Ana generates an image with AI for the reading club card. She checks the terms, does not use it to sell, and mentions that she created it with AI.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open the browser and type "pixabay.com" in the address bar (a site with free-to-use images).
Step 2: In the search box type "mountains" and press search.
Step 3: Look at the page: there are many photos. Notice the tab that shows the licence, usually next to each image.
Step 4: Open an image and read the licence: it says you can use it freely, even in projects, without asking permission.
Step 5: Download an image you like by pressing the download button.
Step 6: Now compare: open any image in Google Images and notice that it says nothing about permission to use. That one is not ours; let us not use it without checking.
Step 7: Write your "personal rule" on a piece of paper: "If it is not clear that I can use it, I ask permission or look for another one".
Step 8: Keep that paper near the computer as a reminder.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is copyright? a) A tax. b) The creator's right to decide how their work is used. c) An app.
2. If I use a photo without permission on my website, what am I doing wrong? a) Nothing. b) I am using another person's work without permission. c) Only if it is in colour.
3. Does "not for profit" mean I can use anything? a) Yes. b) No, the work still has an owner. c) Only songs.
4. What is citing the author? a) Calling them on the phone. b) Mentioning who created the work. c) Paying them.
5. If I generate an image with AI, who can be its author? a) Nobody knows; it depends on each country's and tool's rules. b) Always the cat. c) The first person who sees it.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-a.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Ask permission before using other people's work, even for a non-profit use.
- Respect people's image: do not publish photos of others without their consent.
- Check the terms of AI tools before using their content in public or in a business.
- Do not pass off other people's content as your own. Honesty is the foundation of trust.
- Protect your own authorship: you decide who uses what you create, and do not let others publish your work without permission.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Every work has an author, and the author decides how it is used. To use other people's content we ask permission, cite and read the terms. What we create, even with AI, also has rules. With this we close the "Seed" band of this module. In the next level we will start the "Root" band: we will learn how to give precise instructions to AI to write articles.
