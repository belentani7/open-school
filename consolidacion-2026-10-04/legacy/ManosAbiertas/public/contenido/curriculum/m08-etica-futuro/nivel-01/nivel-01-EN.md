# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 01
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 1 hour

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what personal data is with examples from everyday life.
- Recognize which of our information travels across the internet without us noticing.
- Know why personal data is valuable and why we must take care of it.
- Learn the concept of privacy: the ability to decide who sees what.
- Take the first steps to protect personal information.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Personal data | Any information that identifies us: name, phone, address, photos. |
| Privacy | The ability to decide who can see our information and who cannot. |
| Digital footprint | The trace we leave on the internet with every action: searches, photos, messages. |
| Profile | The "file" that companies build with our data to get to know us. |
| Consent | The permission we give for someone to use our information. |
| Privacy notice | The text that explains what companies do with our data. |

## 📚 Main lesson
At home we keep important documents in a box or a locked drawer: the ID card, the deeds, the family photos. We do not leave them in the street. Personal information is just as valuable, but today it also lives on our phone and on the internet. And there, sometimes, we leave it more exposed than we think.

Personal data is any information that identifies us. Our full name, our date of birth, our address, our phone number, our photos, even what we like and what we search for. All of that is personal data. It is like the key to the house: with the key, someone could come in. With the data, someone could pretend to be us.

We all leave traces without noticing. When we search for a recipe, when we watch a video, when we write a message, marks remain. It is like walking on sand: every step leaves a footprint. The sum of those footprints is our digital footprint. On their own they seem small, but together they say a lot about us.

Companies collect those traces and build a profile: a kind of file with what we like, what we buy, what worries us. With that profile they offer us products, show us ads and sometimes decide things about us. It is as if a market vendor knew all our tastes and knew exactly what to say to convince us.

Privacy is our ability to decide who sees what. Nobody forces us to leave the window open: we choose. The same happens with data, but often we do not realize the window is open because we opened it by accepting terms we do not read, with long sentences full of strange words.

That text we accept when we install an app is called a privacy notice. It explains what the company will do with our data. Many times we accept it without reading it, like signing a contract without looking. You do not need to be an expert: just take a quick look and ask ourselves whether what the app asks for seems reasonable.

Consent is the permission we give. The good news is that, for many things, that permission is ours: we give it or we refuse it. Before installing an app or accepting terms, we can stop and ask ourselves: why does this app need to know my location? Why does it need access to my contacts? Sometimes the answer is reasonable and other times it is not.

It is not about living in fear. It is about living with knowledge. Just as we learn to lock the front door without being afraid of the street, we can learn to protect our data without being afraid of the internet.

A simple rule to remember: if a service is free, it is possible that we are the product. Many "free" apps are funded with our data. It is not bad in itself, but it is good to know. It is like a market where the fruit is free but they take the data from our wallet.

In this level we only begin to look. We will review what information we have on our phone and what permissions we have given without noticing. You do not have to change everything in one day: just start looking. Awareness is the first step of protection.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The recipe and the footprint.** Rosa searches for "recipes for diabetics" on the internet. From that moment on she sees ads for diabetic products. Her search left a footprint and her profile grew.
2. **The flashlight app.** Antonio downloads a free flashlight app that asks for access to his location and contacts. Antonio wonders: why does a flashlight need to know where I live?
3. **The market profile.** Carmen accepts the terms of an online shop without reading them. Months later she receives very personalised emails. The shop built her profile from her purchases.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Get your phone and unlock it.
Step 2: Go into "Settings" or "Configuration" (the icon is usually a gear or a wheel).
Step 3: Look for the "Privacy" or "Permissions" section. Open it.
Step 4: Look at the list of apps and what permissions they have (camera, microphone, location, contacts).
Step 5: Choose an app that does not seem necessary in that list and ask yourself: what do I use it for? Does it really need that permission?
Step 6: Write down on paper which permissions surprise you. Do not change anything yet: just look.
Step 7: Think out loud: is there any app that knows more about me than I thought?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is personal data? a) Only the social security number. b) Any information that identifies us: name, phone, photos. c) Only passwords.
2. What is the digital footprint? a) The traces we leave on the internet with every action. b) A type of shoe. c) The messages we delete.
3. Who decides who sees our information? a) The companies, always. b) Us, with our privacy and consent. c) The government, without exception.
4. Why are many apps free? a) Out of kindness. b) Because they are funded with our data. c) Because they cost nothing to make.
5. Does the privacy notice explain what the company does with our data? a) Yes, and it is worth taking a look. b) No, it never says anything. c) Only in English.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-a.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Our data is valuable and belongs to us: nobody should use it without our consent.
- Companies must explain clearly what they do with our information, not hide it in the small print.
- Letting an app access our data without looking is like signing a blank contract.
- Privacy is a right for everyone, including older people, who are often more vulnerable.
- If something seems odd (an app asking for more than necessary), we have the right to say no.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Personal data is any information that identifies us, and we all leave a digital footprint. Companies use that footprint to build profiles, which is why privacy and consent are so important. Protecting our data is not about fear; it is about knowing what we do. In the next level we will learn the first great protection tool: passwords.
