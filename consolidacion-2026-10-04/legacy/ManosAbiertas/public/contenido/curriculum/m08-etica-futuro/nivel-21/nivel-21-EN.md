# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 21
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Canopy
## Estimated time: 3 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what an ethics committee is and what it is for in an organisation.
- Learn what algorithm auditing is: reviewing machines from the inside.
- Know who takes part in an ethics committee and what questions they ask.
- Learn how the problems of an algorithm are detected and corrected.
- Discover that older people can contribute to ethics committees.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Ethics committee | A group of people that reviews whether AI decisions are fair. |
| Audit | An in-depth review of how a system works. |
| Algorithm | The step-by-step recipe the machine follows to decide. |
| Report | The written document that summarises what was found in the review. |
| Correction | The fixing of the problems detected in the audit. |
| Diversity | The presence of different voices in the committee to see more angles. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Imagine that in a residents' association, important decisions were always made by the same three people, without looking at the consequences. Soon there would be problems: someone would be left without a voice and decisions would be unfair. Organisations, when they use artificial intelligence, protect themselves with two instruments: ethics committees and algorithm audits. Let us get to know them.

An ethics committee is a group of people that reviews whether AI decisions are fair. They are not necessarily technicians: they are people with judgement who ask questions. Does this system treat everyone equally? Who does it harm? What data does it use and where does it come from? Who answers if it fails? The committee does not programme the machine: it looks at it with human eyes. It is like the jury of a building project: it does not build the house, but it judges whether it is well built.

Who forms an ethics committee? Ideally there should be diversity: people from different departments, with different ages, backgrounds and experiences. An engineer, a nurse, a social worker, an older person who uses the service. Each one sees a different angle. The more diversity, the fewer blind spots. It is like a recipe: if all the cooks think alike, the stew always tastes the same and nobody notices the salt is missing.

What exactly does the committee do? It meets, examines the AI systems the organisation wants to use, asks questions, listens to the people affected and issues a ruling: this system can be used like this, this one needs changes, this one should not be used. Its voice is not decorative: if the committee says a system is unfair, the organisation must listen. The committee is the conscience of the organisation.

Algorithm auditing is the other tool. If the committee is the jury, the audit is the technical inspection. An auditor (or a team) reviews the algorithm from the inside: with what data it was trained, whether those data are balanced, whether the result is fair for all groups, whether it can be explained, whether it complies with the law. At the end, they write a report with what was found and propose corrections.

Let us think of the audit like a car service. Nobody gets into a car that has not been serviced for years without distrusting it. With algorithms it is the same: those that make important decisions should undergo periodic reviews. The audit is not to punish anyone: it is to find the faults before they cause harm. A fault detected in time is a fault that did no harm.

What is looked for in an audit? Several things. That the data are not unfair (without biases), that the algorithm does not discriminate, that decisions can be explained, that there is human oversight, that the laws are complied with. The auditor is like the plumber who checks the pipes: they look for hidden leaks that would ruin the wall. And if there are any, they point them out and propose fixing them.

After the audit comes the correction. If a problem has been found, it must be fixed: change the data, adjust the algorithm, add more human control, or withdraw the system if it cannot be fixed. The correction is not always easy or immediate, but it is mandatory. An organisation that ignores the audit report is ignoring the safety net.

And what role can we, older people, have in all this? A very real one. Ethics committees need diversity of ages and experiences. An older person who knows real needs, who has lived through injustice, who knows how people are treated, brings a voice that no engineer can replace. More and more organisations are looking for that voice. Our experience matters in the committee.

In this level we have seen the machinery of justice inside organisations. The ethics committee asks if it is fair; the audit checks whether it works well; and together they protect people. Now we know that when an organisation talks about "AI ethics", it is not smoke: it is people who meet, algorithms that are reviewed and reports that are corrected.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The hospital committee.** In Rosa's hospital there is an ethics committee that reviews the emergency classification system. A nurse on the committee notices that the system undervalues older people's symptoms and they ask for it to be corrected.
2. **The company audit.** Antonio's company commissions an audit of its customer service algorithm. The report detects that it responds poorly to certain accents and they propose adjustments. The algorithm improves.
3. **Carmen's voice.** Carmen, aged 65, is invited to an ethics committee of her city council as a service user. Her experience with medical appointments helps make the system friendlier towards older people.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your notebook and write: "Ethics committees and audits".
Step 2: Write in your own words: what does an ethics committee do? what does an audit do?
Step 3: Ask a free AI chat: "What is an artificial intelligence ethics committee and what questions does it ask?". Read the answer.
Step 4: Also ask: "What is an algorithm audit and why is it important?". Write down two ideas.
Step 5: Write three questions you would ask in an ethics committee (for example: "does this system treat older people equally?").
Step 6: Think of an automatic system you use (appointments, bank). Write it down and write what you would ask its auditor.
Step 7: Reflect aloud: do you think your life experience could contribute to an ethics committee?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is an ethics committee? a) A group of people that reviews whether AI decisions are fair. b) A type of algorithm. c) A works commission.
2. What is an algorithm audit? a) An in-depth review of how a system works. b) A music lesson. c) A home insurance.
3. Why is diversity important in a committee? a) It does not matter. b) Because each person sees a different angle and there are fewer blind spots. c) Only to look good.
4. What happens if the audit finds a problem? a) It is ignored. b) It is corrected: change data, adjust the algorithm or withdraw the system. c) It is hidden.
5. Can an older person contribute to an ethics committee? a) No, that is for engineers. b) Yes, their life experience is valuable. c) Only if they are an IT expert.

Answers: 1-a, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- The ethics committee is the conscience of the organisation: its voice must be heard.
- An honest audit finds problems to correct them before they cause harm.
- Diversity of ages and experiences is a value, not a formality.
- Ignoring an audit report is despising the safety net.
- Our life experience is a treasure for deciding on ethics: let us offer it.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Ethics committees ask whether AI decisions are fair, and audits check from the inside how algorithms work. Diversity avoids blind spots, and correcting faults is mandatory. Older people can contribute their experience to these spaces. In the next level we close the Canopy band with professional deontology: the rules of conduct of those who work with AI.
