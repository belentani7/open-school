# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 12
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand that AI can make mistakes, like any tool.
- Know who is responsible when a machine makes an error: the company.
- Learn the concept of product liability and the chain of command.
- Learn how to complain when an automatic system harms us.
- Discover that the person is always behind the machine.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Responsibility | The obligation to answer for the damage we cause. |
| Manufacturer | The company that designs, manufactures and sells the system or product. |
| Chain of responsibility | The list of actors involved who must answer. |
| System error | A machine failure that produces a wrong result. |
| Damage | The harm a person suffers (economic, physical, moral). |
| Compensation | The financial compensation for damage caused. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Machines are not perfect. A washing machine can break down and soak our clothes. A blender can burn out. With artificial intelligence the same thing happens: it can make mistakes, and its errors can affect people. The important question is: when the machine makes a mistake, who is responsible? The answer, as in the kitchen, is clear: the person who set the recipe in motion is responsible.

When we buy a defective product, the law protects us: we can return it, complain or ask for compensation. With AI something similar happens. If an automatic system harms us (a wrong loan decision, a wrong diagnosis, an improper charge), we are not defenceless: there is a company behind it that must answer. The machine has no pockets: whoever designs, manufactures or uses the systems pays for the errors.

This is called the chain of responsibility. It is like on a construction site: if the roof falls, we do not blame the brick. We ask who designed it, who built it and who supervised it. With AI it is the same: there is a manufacturer (who made the system), an operator (who uses it) and, sometimes, a data controller. Each link has duties.

And what if the error comes from the data? If the machine learned with incomplete or unfair data, whoever chose that data also has responsibility. The excuse of "the machine got it wrong by itself" does not hold: the machine does not learn alone, it learns with what we give it. It is like a pot that burns: we do not blame the pot, but whoever left it on the fire unsupervised.

An important idea is the distinction between two roles: the manufacturer's and the user's. The manufacturer is responsible if the system is badly made: if the machine had a design or data fault. The user (the company using it) is responsible if they use it badly or without oversight. For example, if a company uses a chatbot to handle complaints and the chatbot gives false information, the company is responsible to its customers, even if the fault "is the machine's".

European law will strengthen this with new rules on AI liability. The idea is that victims of machine errors can complain more easily, without having to prove impossible things. It is like the return in a shop: before, you had to prove who broke the product; now, the law makes it easier for the company to answer for what it sells.

And us, ordinary people? We have a very practical role: to complain when something goes wrong. If an automatic decision harms us and they do not explain the reason, we can ask for explanations in writing. If they do not give them, we can file a complaint. Consumer associations help us. We must never think "it is the machine, nothing can be done": something can always be done.

You should also keep evidence. If a system harms us, we note the date, save screenshots of the message or the error, and keep the company's replies. It is like keeping the purchase receipt: without proof, it is harder to complain. With the receipt, the complaint has legs.

A deeper reflection: when the machine makes a mistake, it is not an excuse not to answer. Ethics and law agree on this: responsibility always reaches people. Behind every algorithm there are engineers, managers and companies making decisions. People's dignity demands that someone takes responsibility when something goes wrong.

In this level we have learned not to blame the machines. The machine is the tool; people are the responsible ones. And we, as citizens, have the key to complaint: ask for explanations, keep evidence and complain when something harms us. With that key, technology respects us.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The improper charge.** Rosa receives a charge in the bank she does not recognise. A machine processed it. Rosa keeps a screenshot of the charge, calls the bank and complains. They refund the money: the entity answers for its system.
2. **The loan wrongly denied.** George is denied a loan due to a "system error" that, when reviewed, was a badly copied piece of data. George demands the correction and a written explanation. The company gives it.
3. **The lying chatbot.** Carmen asks a company's chatbot about a refund and it gives her false information. Carmen complains to the company and remembers that the chatbot is not an excuse: the company answers for its machine.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your notebook and write: "My rights when a machine harms me".
Step 2: Write below the three steps: 1) ask for a written explanation, 2) keep evidence, 3) complain.
Step 3: Think of a service you use with automatic decisions: bank, shop, courier. Write it down.
Step 4: Look on that service's website for the "Customer service" or "Contact" section. Note how to complain.
Step 5: Write a complaint message template starting like this: "I am contacting you to request an explanation about the automatic decision that..." and complete it.
Step 6: Search the internet for "consumer association" in your city and write the phone number in your notebook.
Step 7: Reflect aloud: did you know there is always a responsible person behind a machine?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Can artificial intelligence make mistakes? a) No, never. b) Yes, like any tool. c) Only in films.
2. When a machine harms a person, who is responsible? a) The machine. b) The company that designed, manufactured or uses it. c) Nobody.
3. What is the chain of responsibility? a) The list of actors involved who must answer. b) A type of bicycle chain. c) The sequence of steps in a recipe.
4. What do we have to keep before complaining? a) Nothing. b) Evidence: dates, screenshots, the company's replies. c) Only the password.
5. Is it worth complaining if a machine harms us? a) No, "it is the machine". b) Yes, there is always a company that must answer. c) Only if the damage is very big.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Responsibility always reaches people: the machine has no pockets and no conscience.
- The excuse of "the machine made a mistake" does not hold: data and use are decided by people.
- The law makes it easier for victims of AI errors to complain.
- Keeping evidence and complaining is not being annoying: it is defending our dignity.
- When we complain, we also protect others: we force the system to improve.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Machines make mistakes, and when they do, responsibility reaches people: the manufacturer, the user or whoever chose the data. We have the right to ask for explanations, keep evidence and complain. European law is making those complaints easier. In the next level we continue with transparency: the right to know when and how AI acts.
