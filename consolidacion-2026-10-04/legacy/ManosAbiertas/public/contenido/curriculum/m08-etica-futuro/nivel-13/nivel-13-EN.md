# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 13
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what transparency in artificial intelligence is.
- Know that we have the right to know when we interact with a machine.
- Learn to recognise the notices that applications must show.
- Distinguish what is transparent from what is hidden.
- Develop the habit of asking: "did a machine do this?".

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Transparency | The quality of showing clearly how something works, without hiding anything. |
| Interaction | The moment we talk or work with something or someone. |
| Notice | A message that informs us of something important, like a sign. |
| Labelling | The mark that indicates the origin of content (made by AI, for example). |
| Right to know | People's right to know what is behind what they see. |
| Opacity | The opposite of transparency: that something cannot be seen or understood. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In a good house, the pipes are visible or at least you know where they are. If something is hidden behind the walls and nobody knows where, any breakdown is a mystery. Transparency in AI is exactly that: that we people can see and understand what is behind what the machine does, without magic tricks.

Transparency has several faces. The first is knowing when we are talking to a machine. When we call a service and an automatic system answers, we have the right to be told. When a text, an image or a video has been created by AI, it is also good for it to be known. It is not to annoy: it is so that we decide with knowledge.

The European law we saw in level 11 requires that transparency. Companies must warn when we interact with a machine and when content is generated by AI. It is like the "attention, surveillance camera" sign in a shop: it is not a decoration, it is a right to know we are being observed.

Why does knowing there is a machine in front matter so much? Because it changes our trust. If we believe we are talking to a person and it is a machine, we can feel deceived. If a text looks written by a journalist and it is an automatic generator, the information deserves another reading. Knowing the truth of the origin is the first step of trust.

This is called labelling. Just as foods carry a label with their ingredients, content made with AI should carry its mark. There are debates on how to do it well, but the idea is simple: that nobody passes off one thing for another. If we see an image, we want to know whether it is real or generated. That knowledge gives us power to decide.

Transparency also refers to decisions. When a machine makes an important decision about us (a loan, a contract, an admission), we have the right to have the reasons explained to us understandably. Saying "the system decided" is not acceptable. Opacity is the enemy of justice: if we do not see the reasons, we cannot know whether there is an error or an injustice.

How do we detect the lack of transparency? There are signs. When an app does not warn that it is a machine, when an automatic decision arrives without explanation, when content seems real but we do not know its origin, there is opacity. It is like a cook who does not tell us what the stew contains: we can ask and, if they do not answer, suspect.

Our role as citizens is to ask. "Did a machine do this?" is a legitimate and valuable question. When we ask, we force systems to be clearer. Each question from a curious citizen is a small push towards a more transparent digital world. There are no stupid questions: questions are the tools of critical thinking.

We also have to learn to read the notices. Many apps already warn: "this text was generated by artificial intelligence", "this assistant is automatic". Reading those notices makes us informed citizens. It is like reading the food label: it is not boring, it is responsibility.

Transparency does not mean companies must reveal their kitchen secrets. It is not about giving the complete recipe of how an algorithm works, which is their business. It is about what affects us: the origin of the content, the interaction notices and the reasons for decisions. The boundary is clear: what affects the person, the person knows.

In this level we learn to demand light. When something cannot be seen, we ask. When something is opaque, we distrust politely. Transparency is not only a company obligation: it is our right and a habit we practise every time we ask "who is behind this?".

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The assistant that introduced itself.** Rosa calls her phone company and the assistant says: "I am an automatic assistant". Rosa knows it is a machine and decides what to do with that information.
2. **The text label.** Carmen reads an article on the internet and at the end there is a note: "This content was generated with artificial intelligence". Carmen reads it with different eyes and cross-checks it.
3. **The loan without explanation.** George receives an automatic loan denial with no reasons. He writes to the company and asks for an understandable explanation, exercising his right to know.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open a free AI chat in the browser (ChatGPT, Copilot).
Step 2: Type: "Write a paragraph explaining what the right to transparency in AI is".
Step 3: Copy the text into a document or your notebook.
Step 4: Now find a news article about AI and check whether it carries any label or notice of generated content.
Step 5: Go back to the chat and ask it: "Should companies warn when we talk to a machine? Why?". Read the answer.
Step 6: Write in your notebook two examples from your life where you would like to know whether there is a machine in front.
Step 7: Think aloud: what question would you always ask when something seems opaque to you?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Opacity is the enemy of justice: without visible reasons we cannot detect errors or injustices.
- Companies must warn that there is a machine in front: it is a right to know, not a favour.
- Labelling AI-made content protects our trust.
- Asking does not annoy: each citizen's question forces systems to be clearer.
- What affects the person, the person knows: that is the boundary of transparency.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Transparency tells us when there is a machine in front and what origin the content has. We have the right to clear notices, labels and explanations of decisions. Opacity is the enemy of justice, and asking is our tool. In the next level we go up one more step: explainability, or how to make AI decisions understandable.
