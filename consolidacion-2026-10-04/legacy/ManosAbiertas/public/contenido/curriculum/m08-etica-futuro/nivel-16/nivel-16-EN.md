# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 16
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Branch
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what digital rights are: the same rights as always, now also on the internet.
- Learn about the right of access to the internet and technology.
- Know that we have the right to protection of our data and to equality in the digital world.
- Discover the right to digital literacy: learning is not a favour, it is a right.
- Recognise that digital rights protect us all, including older people.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Digital rights | People's rights applied to the world of the internet. |
| Digital literacy | The ability to learn and use technology with ease. |
| Digital divide | The distance between those who have access to technology and those who do not. |
| Universal access | That everyone can reach the internet, regardless of age or money. |
| Digital equality | That technology treats all people equally. |
| Digital citizenship | The condition of being a citizen with rights also on the internet. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We all know we have rights: to health, to education, not to be discriminated against. Those rights do not disappear when we go online. On the contrary: in the digital world, we have specific rights that protect us. They are the digital rights, the bill of rights of digital citizenship. And they are ours, at any age.

What does it mean to be a digital citizen? The same as being a citizen of the neighbourhood, but in the world of screens. We live on the internet: we pay, we book appointments, we talk to the family, we look for information. That is why this world must also respect us. A digital citizen has the right to be there, to participate and to be treated with dignity in the digital space.

The first digital right is access. We live in a time when many procedures are done online: booking an appointment, paying taxes, checking the bank. Whoever cannot access the internet is left out of many things. That is why the right to access the internet and technology is fundamental. It is like the right to the street: without a street, there is no neighbourhood.

From that comes another right: digital literacy. Learning to use the phone, the computer, the apps and artificial intelligence is not a luxury: it is a right. We all have the right to learn, at any age. When an older person says "I am past these things", we must remind them that yes: they have the right to learn, just as they learned to use the landline phone or the microwave.

The digital divide is the distance between those who handle technology and those who do not. It is not only a matter of money: it is also age, place of residence and training. Older people and those in rural areas usually end up on the wrong side of that divide. Digital rights exist so that the divide closes, not so that it grows.

Another essential right is the protection of our data, which we already saw in the Stem band. No one can keep our information as if it were theirs. And no one can use technology to discriminate against us. Digital equality means that a machine cannot treat us worse because of our age, our neighbourhood or our condition. Digital rights prohibit that discrimination.

We also have the right to participate. To give opinions, to report, to ask for explanations, to be informed. The digital space is not only for young people or technicians: it belongs to everyone. Whoever participates in the digital world with rights is a full citizen. Whoever stays out loses their voice.

How do we protect our digital rights? First, by knowing them. Second, by exercising them: asking for explanations, complaining, participating. Third, by learning: every course, every class, every resolved doubt is a conquest. Fourth, by helping each other: when a relative teaches us to do a procedure, they are defending our right to be in the digital world.

Administrations and companies also have obligations. Public websites must be easy to use for everyone, including older people. Services must offer alternatives for those who cannot or do not want to use the internet. An administration that only offers online procedures and leaves many people out is not doing its job well.

In this level we have learned that the internet is not a lawless land. It is another neighbourhood, with rights and duties. We have the right to enter, to learn, to be protected and to participate. And we have the duty to care for those who are still outside the door: by helping them in.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The right to learn.** Rosa, 68, signs up for a free computer course. Someone tells her it is "too late". Rosa remembers that learning is her right and carries on.
2. **The digital counter.** Antonio does not know how to do a procedure online. His daughter helps him create an account and do the procedure, and Antonio understands that helping someone enter the digital world is an act of justice.
3. **The website for everyone.** Carmen finds a very complicated city council website. She writes and asks them to make it simpler: the administration must be accessible to everyone, not only to experts.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your notebook and write: "My digital rights".
Step 2: Write a list of the rights you remember: access, learning, data protection, equality, participation.
Step 3: Ask a free AI chat: "What are citizens' digital rights? Explain them in simple words". Compare it with your list.
Step 4: Think of a procedure you have done online (medical appointment, bank, purchase). Write it down and note: was it easy? Did you need help?
Step 5: Write one right that you think needs more protection in your case.
Step 6: Search the internet for "digital rights charter" and read a summary in a serious medium. Write down one idea that surprised you.
Step 7: Reflect aloud: what will you do to exercise your right to participate in the digital world?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What are digital rights? a) People's rights applied to the world of the internet. b) The rights of machines. c) A type of password.
2. Is learning to use technology a right? a) No, it is a luxury. b) Yes, we all have the right to learn at any age. c) Only for young people.
3. What is the digital divide? a) The distance between those who access technology and those who do not. b) A crack in the screen. c) A broken cable.
4. Must public websites be easy to use for everyone? a) No, only for experts. b) Yes, the administration must be accessible. c) It does not matter.
5. Do you have the right to participate in the digital world? a) No, that is for technicians. b) Yes, participating is a right of digital citizenship. c) Only if you are young.

Answers: 1-a, 2-b, 3-a, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Nobody should be left out of the digital world because of their age: access and learning are rights.
- Helping someone get online is an act of justice, not charity.
- Administrations must make easy websites and offer alternatives for everyone.
- Technology cannot discriminate: digital equality is a right.
- Learning at any age is a conquest that dignifies the person.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Digital rights are the rights we always had applied to the internet: access, learning, protection, equality and participation. Nobody should be left out because of their age, and helping people in is an act of justice. Learning is a right, not a favour. In the next level we will talk about something very visible in the digital world: our identity and our reputation.
