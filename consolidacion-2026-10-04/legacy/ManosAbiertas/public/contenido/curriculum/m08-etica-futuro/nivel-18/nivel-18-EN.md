# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 18
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Branch
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what personal cybersecurity is and why it affects us all.
- Learn the most common scams that arrive by phone, email and messages.
- Learn to recognise scammers who pretend to be relatives or companies.
- Know how to protect the phone, the computer and bank accounts.
- Create a personal security kit to use every day.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Cybersecurity | The protection of our devices and data against attacks. |
| Scam | A deception to take our money or data. |
| Scammer | The person who organises the deception. |
| Vishing | A scam by phone, imitating a bank or company. |
| Smishing | A scam by text message, with fake links. |
| Security PIN | The number that protects the phone or card, like a key. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We protect our house with a lock, and our health with check-ups. And our digital life? It also has its care: it is called personal cybersecurity. It is not a topic for computer experts: it is a topic for everyone. Just as we do not leave the door wide open, we do not leave our accounts and data within anyone's reach either.

Scammers have tricks with their own names. Vishing is the phone scam: someone calls imitating the bank or a company and asks us for data or money. Smishing is the text message scam: a message with a fake link inviting us to click. Phishing we already saw: the fake email. They are all the same recipe with different dishes: imitating someone trustworthy to deceive us.

How does a scammer act? First, they create an emotion in us: fear ("there is a pending charge"), hurry ("your account closes today") or greed ("you have won a prize"). Then, they ask us for something: data, a code, a payment. And finally, they disappear. If we recognise the recipe, we do not eat it. The strong emotion is the sign: when a message or a call upsets us, we must stop and think.

The cruellest trick is the fake relative. Someone calls or writes pretending to be a grandson or nephew: "grandma, I am in trouble, send me money". It is a scam aimed especially at older people that takes advantage of affection. The defence is simple and valuable: never pay without confirming with another relative. Call the mother, the father, the other child: the double-check saves.

What protects our money? First, never give the card PIN, the confirmation code, or the full card number to whoever asks for it by phone, message or email. Neither the bank nor the police ask for those details through those channels. If someone asks for them, it is a scammer. It is like not handing the house key through the window: you do not do it, whatever happens.

The phone and computer are also protected. Putting the PIN or fingerprint on the phone is putting the lock. Not installing apps from strange sites (only from official stores) is not opening the door to strangers. And keeping updates up to date is fixing the leaks. Three small gestures that form a big wall.

There is a key question that protects us: why is this person/company contacting me? If a "bank" suddenly calls us, we do not call anyone: we hang up and we call the number on the card or the official website. It is the call back: verify by the known path, not by the path the stranger tells us.

And if we have already fallen for a scam? First, do not be ashamed: scammers are professionals of deception and anyone can fall for it. Second, act quickly: warn the bank (to try to stop the payment), keep the evidence (messages, phone numbers) and report it to the police. The sooner you act, the more chances of recovering something. And you always have to tell: warning the family prevents the scammer from trying again with others.

Personal cybersecurity is a habit, not a mountain. You do not need to be an expert: the rule of the three "no"s is enough: do not respond to strangers with data, do not click suspicious links, do not pay without confirming. Three "no"s that are learned and repeated, like the three stages of the classic recipe.

In this level we have assembled our personal security kit. We know that scammers imitate voices and companies, that affection can be used against us, and that calm and the double-check are our best defences. With the kit in hand, we walk through the internet with open eyes.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The "bank" call.** Rosa receives a call from someone claiming to be from the bank asking for her PIN "to cancel a strange charge". Rosa hangs up, calls the bank's official number and they confirm it is a scam.
2. **The "nephew" message.** Antonio receives a message from an unknown number: "uncle, I am abroad and my wallet was stolen". Antonio sends nothing: he calls his sister and confirms his nephew is fine.
3. **The fake prize.** Carmen receives a message: "you have won 5,000 euros, click to claim". Carmen does not click: she remembers that prizes are not claimed by clicking links that come out of nowhere.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your notebook and write: "My personal security kit".
Step 2: Write the rule of the three "no"s: do not respond to strangers with data, do not click suspicious links, do not pay without confirming.
Step 3: Check that your phone has a lock: screen locked with PIN, fingerprint or pattern. If it does not, set it now by following the on-screen steps.
Step 4: Write in your notebook your bank's official number (the one on the card or the official website), not the one in messages.
Step 5: Choose the trusted person you would call before doing anything with a stranger (a relative, a friend). Write it down.
Step 6: Ask a free AI chat: "What are the most common scams against older people?". Read the answer and write down one you did not know.
Step 7: Reflect aloud: what would you do if you now received a call asking for your card PIN?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises

2. What is vishing? a) A phone scam that imitates a bank or company. b) A cooking dish. c) A type of wifi.
3. What do we do if someone pretends to be a relative and asks us for money? a) Send it quickly. b) Do not pay and confirm with another relative. c) Send only a part.
4. Does the bank ask for the PIN by phone? a) Yes, sometimes. b) No, never. c) Only if there is a strange charge.
5. If we have already fallen for a scam, what do we do? a) Hide it. b) Warn the bank, keep evidence and report it. c) Keep waiting.

Answers: 1-a, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Shame is the ally of scammers: talking and telling protects the whole community.
- Using the family's affection to deceive is an attack on dignity: reporting it is defending it.
- Never hand over data or money to whoever asks for it in a hurry or with fear.
- Warning our loved ones about new scams is an act of care.
- The three "no"s are not distrust: they are prudence and respect for yourself.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Personal cybersecurity is caring for our digital house: phone lock, three "no"s, double-check and callbacks. Scammers imitate voices and companies, and take advantage of affection. If we fall for it, there is no shame: act quickly, warn the bank and report it. In the next level we close the Branch band with a review and a complete check of our defence.
