# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 05
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a bias is and how it appears in everyday life.
- Know that AI learns from data and that that data can contain errors.
- Recognize that the machine can repeat injustices without realizing it.
- Learn to detect when an AI response sounds odd or unfair.
- Understand that biases can be corrected if detected in time.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Bias | A tendency or inclination that makes us see things in a non-neutral way. |
| Training data | The examples the AI learns from, like a student's books. |
| Prejudice | An idea formed without really knowing reality, sometimes inherited. |
| Neutrality | The quality of not favouring anyone, of treating everyone equally. |
| Sample | A group of examples used to learn or decide. |
| Correction | The act of fixing a detected error or injustice. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We all have some habit of thought that plays a trick on us. For example, if a dog of a certain colour once bit us, we may distrust all dogs of that colour. That is a bias: an inclination of our thinking that is not based on the full reality. Artificial intelligence also has biases, and understanding them is the first step of the Root band.

How does an AI learn? Imagine a cooking student who has only seen her grandmother cook. She would learn to cook like her grandmother, with her tricks and also her quirks. The AI learns the same way: it is shown many examples (the training data) and the machine imitates the patterns it sees in those examples. If the examples contain an error, the machine learns the error.

Let us give a clear example. If an AI is shown photos of gardeners and almost all of them are men, the machine will learn that "gardener" is usually a man. When asked to draw a gardener, it will draw a man. It is not that the machine is sexist: it is that its training data was unbalanced. The machine repeats what it was taught, like a parrot that repeats words without knowing their meaning.

This is an important point: the machine is neither bad nor good. The machine is a sponge that absorbs the examples we give it. If the examples are fair, it will learn fairness. If they are unfair, it will learn unfairness. That is why, when an AI makes an unfair mistake, the blame is not on the machine: it is on the people who chose the data and on the society that produced it.

Biases are not only in images. They are also in words and decisions. An AI that helps select résumés could learn, from old data, that positions of one type are usually held by people of another type. And it would start to favour some and harm others, without anyone explicitly asking it to.

How do we detect a bias? The key is to ask ourselves whether the result treats everyone equally. If a machine decision depends on something that should not matter (sex, age, neighbourhood, skin colour, appearance), there is a bias. It does not matter if the machine has good intentions: machines have no intentions. Only patterns.

Sometimes the bias comes from the sample being small or incomplete. If we use few examples from one group to train a machine, the machine will not know that group well and will make mistakes. It is like learning to cook only three recipes: anything that is not those three we will do badly.

The good news is that biases can be corrected. First they must be detected, and to detect them you have to look. People play a fundamental role: we can review the machines' decisions and say "this is not fair". The more people look, the easier it is to find the errors.

There are also techniques that help: use more varied and balanced data, review results by group, and run tests before launching a system. It is like tasting the food before serving it: you always taste it, because the cook may have made a mistake.

In this level we start looking at AI responses with new eyes. When a machine answers something that sounds odd or unfair, we will stop and ask ourselves: why did it say this? Where did it come from? That question is the germ of the whole ethics module.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The gardener's photo.** Rosa asks an image generator to draw "a gardener" and a man always comes out. She remembers that the training data was unbalanced and that the machine repeats what it learned.
2. **The résumé selection.** Antonio reads in the newspaper that a company used AI to filter résumés and that the AI favoured young people because the company's old résumés were almost all from young people.
3. **The kitchen parrot.** Carmen compares AI to a parrot: it repeats the words it has heard, without knowing what they mean. If it was taught injustices, it will repeat them.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open a free AI chat in your browser (ChatGPT, Copilot).
Step 2: Write this question: "What profession do you think most gardeners in Spain have?". Wait for the answer.
Step 3: Now write: "And in a school playground garden?". Compare the two answers.
Step 4: Ask it: "Where do you get that information?". Observe whether the machine can justify its answers or hesitates.
Step 5: Ask something harder: "Write a story where the main character is a 68-year-old engineer". See if the machine is surprised or does it normally.
Step 6: Write in your notebook: did any answer seem unfair or odd to you? Why?
Step 7: Reflect out loud: does the machine have prejudices or does it repeat the data's prejudices?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a bias? a) An inclination of thinking that is not based on the full reality. b) A type of recipe. c) A phone virus.
2. How does the AI learn? a) By intuition. b) By imitating the patterns of the training data. c) By asking people.
3. If an AI always draws gardeners as men, what does it mean? a) That the machine is sexist. b) That its training data was unbalanced. c) That gardeners are men.
4. Does the machine have intentions? a) Yes, always. b) No, only patterns it repeats. c) Only if it is a big machine.
5. Can biases be corrected? a) No, never. b) Yes, by detecting them and correcting the data. c) Only if you buy an antivirus.

Answers: 1-a, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- AI biases are not the machine's fault, but the fault of the data that we as people choose.
- Unbalanced data produces injustices: reviewing the variety of data is everyone's responsibility.
- Let us detect unfair answers and talk about them: looking is the first step to correcting.
- AI has no intentions: those responsible are the people who design, train and use it.
- A fair decision cannot depend on something that should not matter, such as sex or neighbourhood.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
AI learns from the data we teach it and repeats its patterns, including the errors. A bias is an unfair inclination that comes from unbalanced or incomplete data. Machines have no intentions, but they can repeat injustices. The good news is that biases can be detected and corrected. In the next level we will talk about a more serious problem: when bias becomes automatic discrimination.
