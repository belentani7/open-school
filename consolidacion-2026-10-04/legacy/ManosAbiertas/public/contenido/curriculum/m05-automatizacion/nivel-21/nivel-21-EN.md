## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Business process | A series of work steps that repeat in an organisation. |
| Improve | Make the process faster, cheaper or with fewer errors. |
| Phase | Each stage of the process, from input to output. |
| Input | What enters the process: an order, a request. |
| Output | What comes out: a receipt, a notice, a decision. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we met AI agents. Today we'll apply them to the world of work: business process automation. A business process is a series of steps that repeat in an organisation to do something. For example: receive an order, prepare it, ship it and collect payment. Or in an association: receive a membership request, check the details, register and notify.

Let's think of a bakery counter. The baker receives the order (input), kneads, bakes, charges and delivers (output). It's a process with clear, repeatable steps. If the baker automates part of the process, say the sales accounting, they gain time for what matters: the bread and the customers.

In any organisation there are repetitive processes that can be automated. In an association: member registrations, collecting fees, meeting notices, answering questions. In a small business: orders, invoices, payment reminders, inventory. All of them are chains of steps that repeat and, therefore, can be automated.

The first step to automating a process is to draw it. We already did that in level 14: first the blueprint, then the building. You draw the input, the intermediate steps, the decisions and the output. It's like the baker's map: from raw material to delivered bread. If we don't see the whole process, we don't know where to automate.

The second step is choosing what to automate. Not the whole process: only the steps that are repetitive, that consume time and that don't need human judgement. The value rule we learned in level 1 comes in handy again. The steps that require judgement, personal contact or delicate decisions are left for people.

The third step is building. Here we bring together everything we learned in the module: integration flows to move data, conditions to decide, variables to store, AI to read and write, agents for more complex tasks and bots to answer messages. An automated process usually uses several of these pieces together.

Let's use a real example: member registration at an association. Input: a request arrives through a form. First flow: the request is saved in the sheet and someone is notified. Agent or AI: checks that the data is complete. Condition: if something is missing, ask to complete it; if everything is there, continue. Output: welcome email, notice to the group and payment reminder. Four or five pieces, one single process.

Automating a process has clear benefits: it saves time, reduces copy-and-paste errors and frees people for what matters. But it also has risks, and you have to see them with your eyes open. A badly designed process automates the error and repeats it a thousand times. That's why you test with real data and review.

There's an essential rule at work: automating is not firing. The goal of automation in a small business or an association is to remove the heavy, repetitive work, not to remove people's jobs. The person who did that task moves on to more valuable tasks: serving better, deciding better, caring for customers or members.

Another rule: every automated process has a responsible person. Someone answers for it: looks after it, reviews it, corrects it and updates it. A process without an owner is a garden without a gardener: it grows alone for a while and then fills with weeds. The responsible person is who you ask when something fails.

The documentation we learned in level 14 is essential here. A business process is documented so anyone on the team can understand and continue it. The process doesn't belong to one person: it belongs to the organisation. Documenting it is leaving the recipe written down so nobody depends on a single person's memory.

When you finish this level you'll know how to look at a work process with an automator's eyes: draw it, choose what to automate, build the pieces and name a responsible person. In the next level we'll see a very concrete tool: RPA, the robots that repeat on-screen tasks.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Member registration.** The form fills the sheet, the AI checks the data, the welcome is sent and the group is notified. Three hours of work done in minutes.
2. **Small business invoicing.** When an order is marked as delivered, the flow generates the invoice, sends it to the customer and logs it in the collection book.
3. **Repeated questions.** The bot handles frequently asked questions and routes only the ones that need human contact to a person.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Choose a process from your association, business or work life: for example, "registering a member" or "a customer's order".
Step 2: Draw the process on paper: input, steps, decisions and output. Don't skip any step.
Step 3: Circle the steps that always repeat and don't need deciding: those are the candidates to automate.
Step 4: Mark with a triangle the steps that need human judgement: those stay with a person.
Step 5: Choose a single piece of the process to automate (the easiest): for example, the notice when a request arrives.
Step 6: Build that piece in your integration tool with what you learned: trigger, condition and action.
Step 7: Test with real data and check that the piece fits into the process.
Step 8: Write the documentation for the piece: what it does, who the responsible person is and what to review each month.
Step 9: Note down which piece you'd add next and what savings it would bring.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a business process? a) A series of work steps that repeat. b) A company contract. c) A type of bank.
2. Which steps are automated first? a) The ones that need human judgement. b) The repetitive ones without decisions. c) The most expensive ones.
3. What do you draw before automating a process? a) The logo. b) The process plan, with input, steps and output. c) The budget.
4. Does automating a process mean taking work away from people? a) Yes, that's the goal. b) No, the repetitive part is removed and people do more valuable tasks. c) Only in large companies.
5. Who answers for an automated process? a) Nobody. b) A responsible person who looks after and reviews it. c) The program alone.

Answers: 1-a, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Automate the heavy work, not the people: the goal is to improve work, not eliminate it.
- Processes that handle people's data (members, customers, patients) require care: inform and ask for permission.
- Make sure the process has a human responsible person who answers for what the machine does.
- Don't automate decisions that need empathy: a dismissal, a refusal, a comfort, are human.
- Document and share the process with the team: transparency avoids fear and rumours.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
A business process is a chain of steps that repeats. You draw it, choose the repetitive steps and build the pieces with flows, AI and agents. Every process has a responsible person and is documented. In the next level we'll see RPA: the robots that imitate the manual tasks we do on screen.
