## 📚 Main lesson
In level 20 we met AI agents. In 21 and 22 we applied them to processes and on-screen tasks. Today we climb one more step: we'll coordinate several agents at once, like running a kitchen team. That's called orchestration: making several agents work together, in order, towards the same goal.

Let's think of a restaurant preparing a big menu. It isn't one person doing everything: the chopper, the cook, the plater, the server. For the dish to come out well, you need a head chef who assigns the work and a coordination of timing. Agent orchestration is that digital head chef: it assigns, orders and gathers.

Why several agents instead of just one? Because of specialisation. One agent can be very good at reading documents, another very good at writing texts, another very good at searching data on the internet. Instead of asking one to do everything (and do it halfway), we divide: each agent does its own part, and the result is better than one agent trying everything.

Orchestration has a very clear pattern, similar to a kitchen. First, a coordinator receives the goal: "prepare the monthly newsletter." Second, the coordinator thinks about what tasks are needed and assigns them: "you review the minutes, you summarise the news, you write the text." Third, each agent does its task. Fourth, the coordinator gathers the results, orders them and delivers the complete newsletter.

This pattern is called divide and gather. The big work is divided into pieces, each piece goes to an agent, and at the end the pieces are gathered into the result. It's exactly what a grandmother does organising the Christmas dinner: she assigns the dishes among the relatives, and then everyone sets the table. Divide and gather is the recipe of orchestration.

There are two ways to orchestrate. The first is in a chain: one agent does its task and passes the result to the next, like the assembly line. The second is in parallel: several agents work at the same time on independent tasks, and the coordinator waits for all of them to combine. In a chain for what depends; in parallel for what doesn't. It's like a kitchen: what needs the previous step, in a chain; what's independent, at the same time.

Orchestration also thinks about errors. What happens if an agent fails or delivers something strange? The coordinator must detect it, ask it to repeat or notify a person. A good agent team has controls: every piece is reviewed before moving to the next. It's the supervision from level 20, now applied to the whole team.

To design a team of agents you need to know what each one can do. You don't launch a team at random: you choose the agents according to the task, like choosing cooks according to the dish. A summarising agent to read, a writing agent to write, a search agent to research. Each with its specialty and its tool.

The human part doesn't disappear: it's more important than ever. A person sets the goal, a person sets the limits, and a person does the final review. The agents propose and work; the person decides and signs. Orchestration is a directing craft: knowing what to ask, whom and when to review.

Orchestration is reaching everyday tools. Integration platforms and AI chats already let you create agent teams with buttons, without programming. You define the goal, choose the agents and the system organises itself. It's the perfect time for those of us who keep our lives in order to learn to lead digital teams.

As always in this module, we start small: a team of two or three agents for a concrete task. First you draw the team on paper: who does what, in what order, who gathers. Then you test with sample data. And only at the end do you let it work for real, always with supervision.

When you finish this level you'll know how to design an agent team: assign tasks, choose specialists, coordinate in a chain or in parallel and gather results. In the module's last level we'll see how to look after the whole system: optimisation and maintenance.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The club newsletter.** Agent A summarises the minutes, agent B summarises the news, agent C writes the text and the coordinator combines and delivers the newsletter.
2. **The grants report.** One agent searches for grants, another compares them with the club's requirements and another writes the application. The coordinator reviews the whole.
3. **The annual report.** Three agents review three sections of the accounts and a fourth joins them into a coherent document.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Choose a concrete goal for your agent team: "prepare the monthly newsletter" or "summarise this quarter's meetings".
Step 2: Draw the team on paper: a box for the coordinator and one for each agent with its specialty.
Step 3: Write what you'd give each agent as input (the material) and what it must deliver as output.
Step 4: Decide whether the flow is in a chain (one after the other) or in parallel (at the same time), and draw the arrows.
Step 5: Choose how you control errors: what happens if an agent delivers something strange (it repeats, it notifies a person).
Step 6: In your AI chat or integration tool, try to create the team: define the coordinator and the agents with their roles in the instructions text.
Step 7: Test with a sample material and watch how the tasks are assigned and the result is combined.
Step 8: Review the final result with a critical eye: what would you change about the assignment or the instructions?
Step 9: Write the lesson on your paper: what you gained by dividing the work among several instead of asking one to do it all.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises

3. Why use several agents instead of one? a) To complicate things. b) Because of specialisation: each one does its own part well. c) You shouldn't use them.

5. Who reviews the final result? a) Nobody. b) The first agent. c) A person, always.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- An agent team multiplies speed and error: without human supervision, a small failure becomes big in seconds.
- Define the team's limits: what each agent must not do and what data it must not touch.
- Agents can repeat biases from their training data: review their decisions, especially with people.
- Don't create agent teams for tasks that require empathy: human direction is irreplaceable.
- Document who is responsible for the team and what each agent does: transparency is part of the craft.
