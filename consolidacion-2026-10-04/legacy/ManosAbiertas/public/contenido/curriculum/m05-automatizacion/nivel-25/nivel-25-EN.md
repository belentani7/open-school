## 📚 Main lesson
We reach the module 5's last level. In these twenty-five levels we've walked the whole path: from understanding what automating is to designing agent teams and complete architectures. Today we close with what makes all that work last over time: optimisation and maintenance. Because a system is never finished: it's cared for.

Let's think of a vegetable garden. We plant, water and harvest. But the garden doesn't care for itself: you have to pull the weeds, fertilise, prune and pull out what no longer bears fruit. An uncared-for garden fills with weeds and stops producing. Automations are the same: without maintenance, they fill with failures, old data and steps that no longer serve.

The first maintenance habit is measuring. It's not enough to know an automation exists: you need to know if it works and if it's worth what it costs. You look at the log (level 13): how often it runs, how often it fails, how much it costs (in time or AI limits). An automation that runs little and costs a lot maybe isn't worth it. Measuring is like weighing yourself from time to time: knowing where you stand.

The second maintenance question is the hardest: is it still useful? Our lives change: schedules change, people change, priorities change. What we automated a year ago we may no longer need. Asking "would I use it today?" is the best filter. And if the answer is no, it gets retired. Retiring isn't failing: it's cleaning the house.

Optimisation is the next step. Once we know which automations are worth it, we look for how to make them better: faster, cheaper, clearer. Sometimes it's enough to remove a step that's left over, join two flows that do the same thing or swap a condition for a simpler one. Optimising is pruning: removing what's extra so what remains grows better.

A useful rule: before building something new, review what you already have. Many times the problem we want to solve is already half-solved in an old flow. Fixing the existing is usually cheaper than inventing the new. It's like the pantry: before buying more, look at what's in the cupboard.

Maintenance has its own rhythm. One day a month, a calm hour: you review each automation's log, check that the accounts are still connected, update what changed and retire what's left over. That monthly moment is the garden's watering: small, but essential. Putting it in the calendar (level 2) is a good idea.

You also review the things you can't see. The permissions: is that volunteer's account who left still connected? Remove it. The passwords: do they get changed when someone leaves? Change them. The documentation: is it up to date? Update it. Maintenance isn't only technical: it's also human and security-related. A clean system is a safe system.

And the moment comes to look back. In this module we've learned many things: the if-then rule, reminders, filters, integrations, AI in flows, voice assistants, the automatic home, bots, agents, RPA, architecture and orchestration. It's a huge path. Each person will have done it at their own pace, and that's fine: the module isn't a race, it's a garden.

The most valuable thing of all isn't the tools: it's the judgement. Now you know how to look at a task and ask yourself if it can be automated, with which tool, what limits to set and how to care for it. That judgement doesn't depend on any application: it's yours, and it stays with you even if all the technologies change. Technology passes; judgement remains.

You've also learned the measure of things: automate what helps, leave on paper and in conversation what doesn't. The best automation is the one you don't notice, the one that works silently while we live. And the best person running it is the one who knows when to turn it on and when to turn it off. That, in the end, is the wisdom of the craft.

You take from this module the ability to make your life a little lighter, your work a little more ordered and your community a little better served. And most importantly: with the confidence that technology serves you, not the other way around. You set the goals, you set the limits and you decide.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The club's monthly review.** One hour a month, the coordinator reviews the logs, removes the old flows and updates the volunteers' accounts.
2. **Saving on AI.** The club measured its AI summaries: they used them little. They reduced the requests and saved their monthly limit for what matters.
3. **The clean garden.** The association retired three automations that no longer served and kept five, all cared for and documented.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open your integration tool and go into your scenarios' log.
Step 2: Make a paper list of all your active automations and note, for each one: when it runs, how many times and how many times it fails.
Step 3: Mark each automation with one of three signals: "I use it and it works", "I use it half-way" or "I no longer use it".
Step 4: For the ones you no longer use, decide: retire or pause. Retiring cleans; pausing keeps it just in case. Choose and do it.
Step 5: For the ones you use, think of a small improvement: remove a step, join two flows or simplify a condition. Do it if it's easy.
Step 6: Review the connected accounts: remove the ones you no longer need, especially those of people who no longer take part.
Step 7: Update the documentation of what you changed today.
Step 8: Put next month's review in your calendar, with an hour reserved.
Step 9: Write on your paper the three most valuable things you take from the whole of module 5.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Maintaining the system is also maintaining responsibility: an abandoned system can cause harm with nobody watching it.
- Before retiring an automation, notify the people who use it: don't leave anyone without notice.
- Change passwords and remove the access of those who leave the organisation: security hygiene is a duty.
- Measure the value, but not only in money: also in time, in peace of mind and in the good of the community.
- The technology you care for serves you; the one you abandon serves you poorly. Care is an act of respect.
