## Language: EN · Difficulty: Branch

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what it means for an AI to be multimodal.
- Learn about the machines' "senses": seeing, hearing, reading, and speaking.
- Apply multimodal AI to daily life: plants, documents, languages.
- Recognize its limits and its errors.
- Test in practice a tool that sees the world.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Multimodal | Combining several "senses" at once: text, image, sound, and video. |
| Computer vision | The machine's ability to interpret images and videos. |
| Speech recognition | The ability to turn what we say into text. |
| Transcription | Turning an audio (a meeting, a message) into written text. |
| OCR | Extracting text from an image or scanned document. |
| Modality | Each type of information: text is one modality, image another, sound another. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Human beings use several senses at the same time: we see, hear, touch, read. When you are introduced to someone, you look at them, hear them greet you, and read their lips. For a long time, artificial intelligence was "single-sense": it only read, only listened, or only looked. Now multimodal models are arriving, which combine several senses at once.

"Multimodal" means that one same system can receive text, image, sound, and video, and understand them together. It is like a person who can read a sign, hear a question, look at a photo, and answer by explaining what they see. That mix of senses is what is changing our relationship with technology.

Think of the most useful daily example: the phone camera. You no longer need to type "what plant is this?" in a search engine. You take a photo of the plant, and the machine looks at it, compares it with millions of images, and answers: "it is a snake plant". That is computer vision combined with knowledge.

Another everyday example: traveling and not understanding the menu's language. With the phone camera pointed at the menu, the machine reads the text (OCR), translates it, and overlays the translation. In seconds, a foreign document becomes something understandable. It is reading, vision, and translation working together.

There is also the machines' ear. Speech recognizers turn what we say into text: that is how we dictate messages without typing, how assistants understand us, and how meetings get transcribed. There are open tools, like Whisper, that transcribe almost any audio with astonishing fidelity.

And then there is the reverse sense: the machine speaks. Multimodal models not only understand images, they also describe them. A person with vision problems can point the phone at a scene and hear: "there is a table with three cups and a cat sleeping beside it". Accessibility is one of the greatest gifts of this technology.

Multimodal AI also understands videos. It can watch a video, hear what is said, and summarize what it is about. That helps with studying, reviewing recorded meetings, or understanding content in another language. It is like having a very attentive person who has watched everything for us.

However, you must know the limits. Models see images, but they do not see them like we do: they look for patterns. They can be wrong, confuse a strange object, or "see" things that are not there. If the photo is dark or blurry, the error grows. The machine's vision is powerful, but not infallible.

An important limit is privacy. When you upload a photo to be identified, that image travels to servers. It is worth knowing this and not photographing personal documents or people without permission. The camera that helps you can also be a window into your life.

Another limit: combining senses also allows more sophisticated deception. We already saw deepfakes in level 14; now an AI can see, hear, and speak with someone's voice, which makes fraud harder to detect. The critical eye we learned is still essential.

Even so, the balance is very positive for people over 40. Multimodal AI removes barriers: the language barrier, the vision barrier, the reading barrier, the technology barrier. A person who never used a computer can now point with the camera and get answers. It is technology that brings close, not pushes away.

Think of all the tasks you can already do with your phone: identify a plant, translate a sign, transcribe a conversation, describe a photo for someone, read a scanned document. Each of those tasks used to be for specialists. Now they are within reach of a camera.

The new skill you must cultivate is knowing when to trust what the machine sees and when to verify. To identify a plant, trust it. For important decisions about documents or health, confirm with reliable sources. The machine is an excellent first opinion, but not the last word.

In the next level, we will go deeper into a topic we already touched in the Seed band: recommendation systems, now with the critical eye we have developed.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Plant identification:** photograph a plant and get its name, its care, and whether it is toxic.
2. **Camera translation:** point at the menu in another language and read the translation overlaid in real time.
3. **Accessibility:** a person with vision problems points at a scene and hears a spoken description.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Take your phone and open Google Lens (inside the Google app or the camera, depending on the model).
Step 2. Point at an object in your house (a fruit, a plant, a tool) and press to identify it.
Step 3. Look at the answer: name, information, and links. Did it get it right?
Step 4. Now point at a text on paper or a label. Check how it extracts the text (OCR) and whether it recognizes it well.
Step 5. If you have a page in another language, point at it with the translation feature and look at the overlaid translation.
Step 6. Ask the text assistant (ChatGPT or Gemini) to describe a photo you upload: "describe this image in three sentences".
Step 7. Compare the description with reality: what did it get right? What did it invent?
Step 8. Write a conclusion: what are the advantages and risks of a machine "seeing"?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What does it mean for an AI to be multimodal?
2. Give two examples of AI that use sight and two that use hearing.
3. What is OCR and what is it for in daily life?
4. What important limit does machine vision have?
5. Why is privacy a risk when using the camera with AI?

**Answers:** 1) That it combines several senses at once: text, image, sound, and video. 2) Sight: identifying plants and translating signs with the camera. Hearing: dictating messages and transcribing meetings. 3) It is extracting text from an image or scanned document; it is used to read labels, menus, or papers. 4) That it looks for patterns, it does not see like us: it can be confused, especially with dark or blurry photos. 5) Because the photos travel to servers; it is best not to photograph personal documents or people without permission.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Multimodal AI opens windows into our lives: every photo and every audio we upload is processed on servers. You must protect your own privacy and others'. On the other hand, it is a huge inclusion tool for people with disabilities or low digital literacy. And because it combines senses, it also multiplies deception: the critical eye and verification are still essential.

## 🔓 Open tools
- **Google Lens** (lens.google): identify objects, plants, and texts with the camera.
- **Google Photos** (photos.google.com): search your photos by content ("beach", "cat") using computer vision.
- **Whisper** (openai.com/whisper): open and free audio transcription.
- **Seeing AI** (seeingai.microsoft.com): Microsoft's app that describes the world for people with vision problems.
- **Hugging Face Spaces** (huggingface.co/spaces): test models that describe images or transcribe audio.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- Multimodal means combining text, image, sound, and video.
- The phone camera already identifies, translates, and reads for you.
- Machine vision looks for patterns: powerful but not infallible.
- Accessibility is one of its greatest gifts.
- Privacy and verification are still essential.
