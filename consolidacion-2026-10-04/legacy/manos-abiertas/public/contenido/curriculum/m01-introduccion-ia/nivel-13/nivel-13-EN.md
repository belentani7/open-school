## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a large language model (LLM) is.
- Understand that these machines predict the next word, one after another.
- Recognize that they sometimes invent answers (hallucinations) and why that happens.
- Know what you can ask them and what you should verify.
- Try a free text assistant in practice.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Language model | A machine trained to read and continue text, like the phone's autocorrect but much bigger. |
| Token | A small piece of text (a word or part of it) that the machine handles as a unit. |
| Prediction | The machine's bet on the word most likely to come next. |
| Hallucination | When the machine invents a false answer with total confidence. |
| Context | Everything you tell the model so it can answer; the more context, the better the answer. |
| Parameters | The internal "screws" that the model adjusted by reading millions of texts. |

## 📚 Main lesson
You surely know your phone's autocorrect: you type a few letters and the phone offers the next word, or even completes whole sentences. Now imagine that autocorrect multiplied by millions of books, articles, news stories and conversations. That is, in essence, a large language model.

In English they are called LLMs, for "Large Language Model". "Language" because they work with words and text. "Model" because they are a simplified version of something real (here, of human language). And "large" because they were trained with amounts of text that would not fit in this room.

How do they learn? The same way as autocorrect: by reading a huge amount of text and learning which words usually come after which. After "good morning, how", the most likely word is "are". The machine does not understand like we do: it calculates probabilities. But by calculating them on a massive scale, the result looks a lot like understanding.

Think of a game of poker with words. Each time the machine must write, it looks at the previous words and bets on the next one. It writes one, looks again, and bets another. That way, word by word, it builds sentences, paragraphs, and entire texts. What looks like a miracle is, deep down, one bet after another.

That way of working explains its two great virtues and its biggest flaw. The virtue: it can write on almost any topic with natural, fluent language. The flaw: because it only bets on what is "likely", it sometimes invents things that look true but are not. That is called hallucination.

Hallucination is like when a neighbor tells an anecdote with great confidence even though they did not live it. The model has no bad intention: it simply put together words that sound good together. If you ask it about something little known, it may "fill in" with false information that sounds plausible.

An example: ask an assistant about an invented event, like the "Sugar War of 1842". Because it sounds like something that could exist, the model may answer with dates, places, and names that never happened. It is not a lie with malice: it is a bet that went wrong.

That is why the golden rule with these models is: what they write is not always true. They are wonderful for drafting, summarizing, translating, correcting, or explaining concepts. But for important data (dates, figures, quotes, news) you must verify with a reliable source.

Another thing to keep in mind: the context. The clearer and more complete what you tell it, the better it answers. If you give it half a sentence, it makes half a bet; if you give it background and details, it makes much better bets. It is like talking to a very clever person who only knows what you tell them.

It is also worth remembering that the model learned from texts made by people, with their successes and their prejudices. It can repeat stereotypes or errors that were in the original texts. It is not a source of supreme truth: it is a mirror of the language that humanity has written.

What can you ask it? An enormous amount: summarize a long document, explain a medical term in simple words, write a letter, translate a recipe, prepare questions for a meeting, or practice a language. It is a conversation helper, not an oracle.

What should you avoid? Giving it personal or banking data, telling it secrets, or asking it for health, money, or legal advice expecting it to be infallible. For that there are professionals, and the model is only an aid to think.

These models are in the assistants we use every day: the phone chat, autocorrect, translators, automatic meeting summaries. They are closer and closer to our lives, and understanding how they work takes away our fear and gives us power.

In the next level, we will meet its most creative cousin: generative AI, which not only writes words but also creates images, sounds, and videos.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Phone autocorrect:** it offers the next word based on what you typed; a language model is that, but with millions of texts.
2. **Document summary:** paste a long text into the assistant and ask for a five-line summary; it saves reading time.
3. **Translation:** write a recipe in English and the assistant translates it into French, word by word.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open the browser and go to a free assistant such as ChatGPT (chatgpt.com) or Gemini (gemini.google.com).
Step 2. Type: "Complete this sentence: the sun rises in the..." and watch how it ends it.
Step 3. Now ask: "What is photosynthesis? Explain it as if I were 60 years old and had never studied science."
Step 4. Check that the explanation is clear and then verify it on Wikipedia: does it match?
Step 5. Now the hallucination test. Ask: "What was the result of the 2075 World Cup?" (or a future event that does not exist).
Step 6. Watch how it answers with confidence even though it could not have watched the match. That is a hallucination.
Step 7. Ask it to summarize this same lesson in three sentences. Compare with what you understood.
Step 8. Write a conclusion: when to trust the assistant and when to verify?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What does LLM mean and what does this machine do, in essence?
2. Explain with the "word poker" idea how the model writes.
3. What is a hallucination and why does it happen?
4. Why is it important to give the model good context?
5. What two things should you avoid when using a text assistant?

**Answers:** 1) Large Language Model: a large language model that predicts the next word. 2) It looks at the previous words and bets on the next one, one after another. 3) It is when it invents a false answer with confidence, because it only puts together likely words. 4) Because the clearer the information you give it, the better its bets and its answer. 5) Avoid giving it personal or banking data, and do not trust health, money, or legal advice without verifying.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Hallucination turns the LLM into a powerful but delicate tool: it can spread misinformation with a voice of authority. That is why it should not be used as a source for news, quotes, or medical data without verification. Also, everything you type into it can be used to train future models: do not share secrets or other people's data. And because it repeats the prejudices of the texts it was trained on, it is wise to read its answers with the same critical judgment you would apply to a stranger.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- An LLM predicts the next word, one after another, with enormous fluency.
- It does not understand like us: it bets on what is most likely.
- It can invent false answers with confidence: those are hallucinations.
- The better the context we give it, the better the answers we get.
- Verifying with reliable sources is the golden rule.
