# Module 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — Level 12
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem
## Estimated time: 2.5 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a bias is and where it comes from.
- Understand that AI learns the prejudices and errors of its own data.
- Recognize examples of algorithmic discrimination in real life.
- Distinguish between a machine failure and a failure that comes from the data.
- Test in practice how unbalanced data produce biased answers.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Bias | A leaning or prejudice: when the system favors a group or an idea without reason. |
| Training data | The information given to the machine so it can learn. |
| Algorithmic discrimination | When the system treats a group of people unfairly. |
| Model error | A wrong answer the system gives even though it is well programmed. |
| Verification | Checking whether an answer is correct before believing it. |
| Data balance | That all groups are represented fairly in the information. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we saw how machines learn. But there is a subject we cannot leave behind: machines also make mistakes, and sometimes they repeat our own prejudices without knowing it. This level is about that: the biases and errors of AI.

Think of a cake recipe. If the flour is in bad condition, the cake will come out badly, even if the cook is the best in the world. The same happens with AI: the machine learns from the data we give it. If the data carries prejudice, the machine will repeat it as if it were true.

A bias is a leaning toward one side without reason. Having preferences is not bad; what is bad is when an important decision favors a group without reason. And that can happen without anyone wanting it, just because the data was unbalanced.

Let us give an example from the working world. If for years a company has hired more men than women for a position, the data in its files reflect that. If it trains an AI to select resumes, the machine will learn that "good resumes" are those that resemble men's, and it will end up discarding women with the same qualifications. Nobody programmed that discrimination: it learned it from the data.

Another example, at the bank. If a loan system learns with data from an era when credit was denied to a neighborhood, it will keep denying it to the people of that neighborhood, even if today they are solvent. The system has no bad intention: it has bad data.

The same happens with facial recognition. There are studies that showed that some cameras failed more at identifying dark-skinned faces than light-skinned ones. The reason is not the camera: it is that they were trained mostly with photos of light-skinned people. The machine knows best the ones it has seen most.

Even in health, biases have been seen. There are systems that help doctors and that, trained with data from only one part of the population, do not detect illnesses well in other people. What is good for some is not good for all, and the data must represent everyone.

The central idea of this level can be summarized with an image: AI is a mirror of its data. If society has prejudices, the data has them, and the machine returns them as if they were laws. It is not that the machine is racist or unfair by its own decision: it is that it learns what we show it.

That is why it is said "garbage in, garbage out". If the input information is bad or incomplete, the answer will also be bad or incomplete. This is not a rare defect: it is the rule. Knowing this gives you an enormous advantage when reading the news and using any tool.

Besides biases, machines have normal errors. Sometimes the data is mislabeled, like a photo of a cat marked as "dog". Other times the case is so rare that the machine never saw it, and when it finds it, it makes a mistake. And other times the system "invents" information to answer, as we will see in detail when we talk about language models.

So, what can we do? First, do not believe everything a machine says. Second, verify important information with other sources. Third, when a tool gives a strange or unfair result, ask: what data did it learn from? Does it represent everyone?

On the creators' side, there is more and more care: the data is balanced, the systems are audited, and diverse teams are formed to review them. It is not perfect, but there is more awareness than ten years ago. And user pressure helps.

You, as a person, have a simple and powerful role: to distrust politely. When a system seems unfair to you, comment on it, ask, write. Companies change when customers ask for it. AI improves when we demand that it be fair for everyone.

To finish, a sentence to remember: AI has no prejudices of its own; it repeats ours. That is why cleaning the data is almost as important as building the machine. In the next level, we will take the leap to one of the most famous intelligences: the large language models, like the ones behind text assistants.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Recruitment:** a company trained its resume filter with old files and the AI discarded women; the problem was in the data, not in the program.
2. **Credit approval:** a bank denied loans to a neighborhood because historical data denied them; the system repeated the past.
3. **Facial recognition:** a camera failed more with dark-skinned faces because it was trained with few photos of that type of skin.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Prepare a bowl with beans: 90 of one color and 10 of another color. That bowl will be your "data set".
Step 2. Take out 20 beans at random, one at a time, and write down the color. You will have taken out mostly beans of the majority color.
Step 3. Now act as the machine: if you are shown a new bean, what color will you say it is? Almost always you will say the majority one.
Step 4. Check that the "machine" has no prejudice: it only learned that the majority color is normal. The prejudice was in the bowl.
Step 5. Open the browser and go to a free image search engine (for example, bing.com/images).
Step 6. Type the word "director" and look at the photos. Now type "secretary" and look again.
Step 7. Compare: the images show what the data that society has produced look like. What did "the machine" learn from us?
Step 8. Write a conclusion sentence: how does data imbalance influence the answers of AI?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Explain with the cake metaphor why a biased system is not only the machine's fault.
2. What is algorithmic discrimination? Give an example.
3. Why did some cameras fail with dark-skinned faces?
4. What does "garbage in, garbage out" mean?
5. What three things can you do as a user when you get a strange or unfair result from an AI?

**Answers:** 1) If the flour (the data) is in bad condition, the cake (the result) comes out badly: the machine repeats what it learns. 2) It is when the system treats a group unfairly because of the data; for example, discarding women's resumes. 3) Because they were trained mostly with light-skinned photos and they know best the ones they have seen most. 4) That if the input information is bad or incomplete, the answer will also be. 5) Do not believe everything the machine says, verify with other sources, and ask what data it learned from or report the injustice.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Bias is not a minor technical flaw: it can deny a loan, a job, or a diagnosis to real people. The responsibility is not the machine's, but that of those who build it and of the society that produces the data. That is why we must demand transparency (that they explain what it learned from), balanced data, and independent audits. As a citizen, asking and complaining when a system is unfair is also a form of care.

- **YouTube** (youtube.com): search "bias in artificial intelligence" to see real examples.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- AI repeats the prejudices that are in its data.
- A system without bad intention can discriminate without knowing it.
- "Garbage in, garbage out": bad data, bad answers.
- Verifying and asking are your best tools as a user.
- Balanced data and audits help reduce bias.
