# Module 1: Introduction to Artificial Intelligence — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand why platforms recommend movies, music, and products to us.
- Recognize the AI behind the "suggestions" of Netflix, YouTube, Spotify, or Amazon.
- Know what data AI uses to get to know us.
- Understand the danger of the "filter bubble".
- Make your own decisions even when the machine suggests something.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Recommendation | A suggestion a platform makes to us: a movie, a song, or a product. |
| Recommendation system | The part of AI that decides what to show us, based on what we do. |
| User profile | The digital portrait platforms build of us from our tastes. |
| Filter bubble | When we only see content similar to what we already like and lose variety. |
| History | The record of what we have watched, listened to, or bought before. |
| Rating | The score or "like" we leave on a movie, song, or product. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Open your favorite movie platform and look at the first row: "Recommended for you". How does the platform know that you like comedies with family drama? It is not magic: it is artificial intelligence studying your history. Every time you watch a movie, finish it, like it, or search for a title, AI takes note. Over time, it builds a portrait of your tastes: a "user profile".

The recommendation system works like a good store clerk. That clerk knows you: he knows what you bought, what you looked at without buying, what you returned. When you come in, he shows you what he thinks you will like. The difference is that the platform's clerk never sleeps, remembers millions of customers, and updates the portrait with every click.

How does it decide? There are several techniques, but they all come down to two ideas. First: "people like you also watched...". If thousands of people who watched the same series as you also watched a particular movie, it is likely you will like it too. Second: "you liked this, so maybe you will like something similar". If you love the movies of one actor, AI will show you other movies with that actor.

The same happens with music. Spotify and YouTube Music watch which songs you repeat, what time you listen, what you skip. That is how they create automatic playlists that change every week. It is not that they have musical taste: it is that they have learned yours. And in shopping, Amazon shows you "customers also bought", based on the carts of people with tastes similar to yours.

This has a very good side: it saves us time and discovers things we might not look for. Many people have found their favorite series or a product that solved their life thanks to a recommendation. AI, used well, is an excellent trusted advisor.

But it also has a side you need to know: the filter bubble. If AI only shows us what we already like, we end up seeing the same thing forever and we lose variety. It is like always eating the same dish because the cook only serves you that. Variety enriches us, and it is wise to step out of the bubble from time to time.

Moreover, the recommendation does not always seek our well-being: it seeks to keep us on the platform. The longer we stay, the more ads are seen and the more money is made. Sometimes AI shows us exaggerated or repetitive content just to keep us hooked. It is worth remembering: the platform wants our attention.

Another detail: AI also learns from people similar to us, not only from us. That is why sometimes it recommends something we do not like at all and we do not understand why. It is because it focused on the group, not on you. Do not take it personally: it is just statistics.

And what about advertising? When you see ads online for things you talked about with your family, it is not that your phone "listens" (although sometimes it does, due to apps' carelessness). Normally, AI crosses your purchase, search, and visit history to guess what interests you. Everything you do on the internet leaves a trace that is used for recommendations.

The good news: you decide. A recommendation is a suggestion, not an order. You can ignore it, search for whatever you like, and rate what you see so AI learns better. The "not interested" buttons exist precisely to fine-tune the profile in your favor.

You can also clear your history and check the privacy settings of each platform. Many apps let you turn off personalization or delete what they have learned about you. Remember: the user profile is a digital portrait of you, and you have the right to see it and correct it.

The recommendation is, in the end, the most invisible and most used AI: we do not see it, but it decides a good part of what we watch and buy. Understanding it gives us power: we know why we are shown something and we know we can choose something else.

In the next level, we will see how AI organizes our photos, recognizes faces, and helps us keep the family's memories.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Series and movies:** after watching three comedies, the platform shows you "because you watched...", and a new comedy appears that you genuinely like.
2. **Music in the car:** your weekly Spotify playlist includes old songs you loved and had already forgotten.
3. **Shopping:** when you search for a blender, the store shows you compatible accessories and recipes, because other buyers also took them.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open the YouTube app on your phone or computer.
Step 2. Search for a topic you like (cooking, gardening, football) and watch two full videos.
Step 3. Return to the YouTube home screen.
Step 4. Look at the "Recommended" or "Because you watched..." row. Notice that many videos are about the topic you just watched.
Step 5. Tap the three-dot menu on one recommendation.
Step 6. Find the "Not interested" option and tap it.
Step 7. Reload the page. You will see that recommendation disappear.
Step 8. Now understand: every click you make teaches AI, and you can correct it whenever you want.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. How does a platform "know" what we like?
2. Mention the two main ideas that recommendation systems use.
3. What is the filter bubble and why is it wise to leave it?
4. Does the recommendation always seek our well-being? What does it often seek?
5. What can you do so AI recommends better for you?

**Answers:** 1) By studying our history: what we watch, search, rate, and buy. 2) "People like you also watched..." and "you liked this, maybe you will like something similar". 3) When we only see content similar to our tastes and lose variety; leaving enriches us. 4) Not always; often it seeks to keep us on the platform for longer. 5) Rate, use "not interested", clear the history, and search for content on our own.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Platforms build a profile of you without you asking for it, and they use it to capture your attention. That portrait can be very precise and very hard to see. It is your right to review it, correct it, and limit it. In addition, the recommendation can create bubbles that distance us from other opinions and realities. Being aware that the machine suggests but does not decide is the basis of free and responsible use.

## 🔓 Open tools
- **YouTube** (youtube.com): free, with a recommendation system and personalization options.
- **Free Spotify** (spotify.com): music with ads and personalized "Discover Weekly" playlists.
- **Google Discover** (on your Android phone): news and videos that adapt to your interests.
- **Letterboxd** (letterboxd.com): free logging of movies watched, with ratings and recommendations.
- **DuckDuckGo** (duckduckgo.com): a search engine that does not store your history, to leave the bubble.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- Recommendations are made by AI studying our history and that of similar people.
- The user profile is a digital portrait of us that updates with every click.
- The filter bubble limits variety; it is wise to leave it.
- The platform often wants our attention, not our well-being.
- You decide: you can ignore, rate, and correct what AI shows you.
