# Module 5: Automation and Integration — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a reminder is and how it differs from an alarm.
- Create reminders with date, time and repetition on your phone.
- Learn to use your voice to create reminders with the phone assistant.
- Set up a repeating reminder for a task from the level 1 list.
- Review and cancel reminders without fear.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Reminder | A notice that tells you to do something at a specific moment. |
| Alarm | A sound notice at a fixed time, like the alarm clock. |
| Repetition | The reminder coming back on its own every day, every week or every month. |
| Notification | The notice that appears on the phone screen. |
| Voice assistant | The phone programme that understands what you say and obeys. |
| Task list | A written record of the things to do. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we chose a repetitive task and described it step by step. Today we take the first big leap: we are going to ask the phone to remind us of things. A reminder is a notice that tells us "do this" at just the right moment. It is like the relative who calls us to remember to take our medicine.

It is worth understanding the difference between an alarm and a reminder. The alarm rings at a fixed time and asks nothing: it is the seven o'clock alarm clock. The reminder is smarter: it tells us what to do. "Take your medicine", "water the plants", "pay the bill". We can have an alarm at nine and a reminder with a written message ringing too.

Reminders have a great advantage: they don't depend on our memory. Memory is like a sieve: things slip through. The phone never forgets. If we say "remember", it remembers. It is like having a personal secretary who never sleeps.

To create a reminder you need four pieces of data. First, what we have to do. Second, when: day and time. Third, whether it repeats: once, every day, every month. Fourth, whether we want it to warn with sound. With those four pieces, the phone does the rest.

The nicest thing about reminders is repetition. If we have to take medicine at eight every day, there's no need to create thirty reminders. You create one with daily repetition. It is like sowing once and harvesting every day.

There are several ways to create reminders on the phone. The easiest for people who don't manage the phone much is voice. We press the assistant button and say: "Remind me to pay the water bill on the 25th". The phone writes it down on its own, saves it and will remind us that day.

You can also write it by hand in the reminders or notes app, or directly in the calendar. Each phone calls it something different: "Reminders", "Tasks", "Notes". It doesn't matter the name: they all do the same thing, warn us at just the right moment.

When the time comes, the phone rings and a message appears on screen. We can tap it to mark the task as done, snooze it for later or ignore it. Marking it as done gives us a small joy and keeps the list clean.

It's important to learn to cancel or change a reminder. It doesn't matter if we make a mistake: you tap the reminder, edit the time or delete it. It is like crossing off a line on the shopping list. Doing and undoing is part of learning.

Reminders are useful at home and at work. At home: taking medicine, watering plants, taking out the rubbish, congratulating a relative. At work: meetings, deliveries, calls, breaks. Every day there are moments that slip away from us; the reminder catches them.

A very practical idea: create a reminder for something we already forgot once. If one day we forgot to pay a bill, that bill deserves a reminder. The phone turns "I forgot" into "I won't forget".

Don't be afraid to try. You can create a test reminder for ten minutes from now and see how it rings and how it appears. It is the best way to understand it: by experimenting. Technology is learned by touching it, not by reading about it.

Remember that a reminder only warns; you do the task. It is a companion that tells you "look at this", not someone who does the task for you. But that small nudge in time is worth gold, especially on busy days.

When you finish this level you will have your first real reminder working. It will be your own automation, small but yours, working on your phone. The next natural step will be automating your email, which we will see later.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The medicine.** The doctor told Carmen to take her medicine at eight in the evening. She created a reminder with daily repetition and the message "Take the medicine". She no longer depends on her memory.
2. **The water bill.** Ramón forgot to pay the water last month. He created a reminder on the 24th of every month: "Pay the water bill". The phone will always remind him.
3. **The grandson's ceremony.** Marta's grandson graduates on the 15th. She created a reminder one day in advance to buy the gift and another on the day itself to arrive on time.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Unlock your phone and find the voice assistant (on many phones it activates by saying "OK, Google" or pressing the side button, or on iPhone with "Hey Siri").
Step 2: Press the assistant button and say clearly: "Remind me to take my medicine at eight in the evening every day". Wait for it to repeat what it understood.
Step 3: Look at the notice it created on the screen. If it understood well, press "Save" or "Confirm".
Step 4: Now open the phone's reminders app (look for "Reminders" or "Tasks"). You will see the saved reminder.
Step 5: Tap that reminder and check the data: what it says, what time, and the daily repetition. Change it to "only once" if you want.
Step 6: Now create a test reminder for ten minutes from now with the message "Reminder test". Save it and wait for it to ring.
Step 7: When it rings, swipe it or tap it to mark it as done. Check that it disappears from the list.
Step 8: Choose your repetitive task from level 1 and create its reminder with the right date and time. You now have your first automation running.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is the difference between an alarm and a reminder? a) None. b) The alarm rings at a fixed time and the reminder also tells you what to do. c) The alarm is at night and the reminder during the day.
2. What data does a reminder need? a) Only the message. b) What to do, when, whether it repeats and the notice. c) Only the time.
3. Can you make a reminder repeat every day? a) Yes, with daily repetition. b) No, it only rings once. c) Only on Mondays.
4. If I make a mistake in a reminder, what do I do? a) Buy another phone. b) Tap the reminder, edit it or delete it. c) Wait for it to fix itself.
5. Does the reminder do the task for me? a) Yes, it does the whole thing. b) No, it only tells me I have to do it. c) Only if I ask it twice.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-a, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Reminders warn, but they don't decide for you. The responsibility of doing the task remains yours.
- Don't set reminders that bother others: a notice during sleeping hours or in a meeting can be uncomfortable. Adjust the schedule.
- If you share the calendar with the family, review which reminders are visible to everyone and which you prefer to keep private.
- Reminders about health or money data must be handled with care: don't share those notices with strangers.
- Use reminders to ease your load, not to add more hurry than necessary.
