# Module 6: Education with AI — Level 06
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a summary is and what it is for when studying.
- Learn to ask AI to summarise long texts.
- Learn the types of summary: short, with key points and with examples.
- Learn to ask for different lengths depending on the use we want.
- Use summaries to review without having to read everything again.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Explanation in simple words |
|---|---|
| Summary | The short version of a text: the essentials, without the extras. |
| Key points | The most important ideas, like the legs of a table that hold it up. |
| Length | How long a text is or how many words it has. |
| Main idea | The idea that sums up everything else, like the trunk from which the branches hang. |
| Review | Looking at the essentials again so we do not forget them. |
| Prompt | The instruction we give to AI: the written order of what we want. |

## 📚 Main lesson
When we study a long topic, we sometimes drown in a sea of words. The summary is the board that keeps us afloat: it gathers the essentials and leaves out what we do not need to remember. Summarising is not cutting at random: it is keeping the trunk and letting the small branches go.

Human beings summarise without noticing. When we tell the family about a film, we do not tell them the forty minutes of cars: we tell them the idea. When we explain to a neighbour how to make a stew, we say the important steps and not every detail. That is summarising, and we have always done it.

AI is an expert at summarising. We give it a long text and it returns the essentials. It is like having someone who read the book for us and tells us what it is about. But beware: its summary will only be good if we ask for it well. This is where the prompt comes in, the order we write to it.

A prompt for summarising has three parts: what we give it, how we want it and what it gives back. Example: "Here is a text about [topic]. Give me a summary of five lines with the most important thing". AI understands the task, the form and the length. It is like asking at the greengrocer's: "give me two kilos of tomatoes, ripe, for salad".

How long should a summary be? It depends on what we want it for. If we only want to find our bearings, three or four lines are enough. If we are going to review, half a page with points is better. If it is to explain to someone, a summary with examples. We can ask: "3-line summary", "10-line summary", "summary with 5 points".

The summary with points is very useful for studying. We ask: "Give me a summary in 5 points". AI returns a short, clear list. That list fits in the notebook, is quick to review and is perfect for the day before an explanation. It is like having the notes of the neighbour who went to the class we missed.

We can also ask for the summary "with an example per point". Thus, each important idea comes with a concrete case that makes it easier to remember. Because, as we saw, our memory clings to stories and examples, not to loose words.

A very good technique: ask for the summary and then ask for "what is left out". That is: "Tell me which important ideas are not in the summary". That way we discover if the text had more things than we thought. It is like emptying the pantry: we see if something important is left at the bottom.

For complicated topics, we can ask for "a summary by chapters" or "by sections". If the text has sections, AI can summarise each one separately. Then we join the mini-summaries and we have the complete map of the topic. It is like assembling a puzzle: first the corners, then the centre.

Beware of one danger: the summary may leave out something important. That is why, when the topic is delicate, we compare the summary with the original: did it say everything essential? Not out of distrust, but out of prudence. The summary is a shortcut, but the shortcut must lead to the same place.

The summary is also useful for studying better with spaced memory. Instead of reviewing the whole text, we review the summary. When we already master it, we erase details and keep only the main idea. Thus, little by little, the summary becomes an outline, and the outline becomes a memory.

And if the text is our own notebook? We can copy our notes into AI and ask it to organise and summarise them. It is like asking an assistant to tidy the pantry and tell us what we have. Our notebook gains clarity and becomes a much more useful tool.

Let us not forget that summarising is also a way of thinking. When we ask AI for summaries, we learn to focus on the essentials, a skill we will use all our lives. In time, we will be able to summarise on our own, without anyone's help.

In this level we will practise with a real text: a short article, a book chapter or our own notes. We will summarise it in three different ways: in three lines, in five points and with examples. We will compare the three summaries and choose the one that best helps us to review.

Remember: the summary is not the enemy of the text. It is its friend. A good summary is born from a good text, and a good student uses both: he reads the original when he wants to go deeper and the summary when he wants to remember.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The summarised recipe.** Marta found a very long article about making bread. She asked AI: "Summarise it in 5 points". Now she reviews the 5 points before kneading.
2. **The chapter review.** Pedro read a chapter about his homeland. He asked: "3-line summary and then 5 points with an example each". With that, he remembered everything without rereading the twenty pages.
3. **The organised notes.** Ana copied her messy notes into AI and asked: "Organise and summarise them". Her notes went from a tangle to a clear ladder.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Choose a text you want to study: a news article, a chapter or your own notes.
Step 2: Open your AI assistant and copy (or write by hand) the beginning of the text in the message.
Step 3: Write the prompt: "This is a text about [topic]. Give me a summary of 3 lines".
Step 4: Now ask: "Now give me a summary in 5 points".
Step 5: Ask for a third summary: "Give me the 5 points with an example from daily life in each one".
Step 6: Compare the three summaries. Which one helps you most to review?
Step 7: Copy the best one into your study notebook with the date and the title of the text.
Step 8: Keep it and use it tomorrow to review without opening the original text.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a summary? a) Copying the whole text. b) The short version with the essentials. c) A drawing.
2. What three parts does a good prompt for summarising have? a) What we give, how we want it and what it returns. b) Greeting, goodbye and signature. c) None.
3. What is the summary in 5 points for? a) To review the essentials quickly. b) To waste ink. c) For nothing.
4. Can we ask for a summary with examples? a) Yes. b) No, never. c) Only on Mondays.
5. What do we do if we suspect the summary left something out? a) We ignore it. b) We compare it with the original. c) We get angry.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-a, 4-a, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- A summary must not distort the text: if you simplify it, do not change its meaning.
- Do not sign an AI summary as your own without reading and understanding it.
- For important data (dates, figures, laws), the summary is not enough: check the original.
- If you share a summary, say it was made by AI: transparency is honesty.
- The summary helps you review, but truly understanding it is your responsibility.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
The summary is the short version of a text: we keep the trunk and let the branches go. With a good prompt (what we give, how we want it and what it returns) AI summarises in lines, in points or with examples. The summary is the shortcut for reviewing, as long as we compare it with the original when the topic is important. In the next level we will learn the reverse path: asking AI to explain concepts, not only to summarise them.
