# Module 6: Education with AI — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand how memory stores what we learn, like a house with several rooms.
- Learn about short-term and long-term memory and why reviewing strengthens it.
- Learn the trick of "spaced repetition": studying several times with breaks in between.
- Discover why sleeping and resting help us keep what we have studied.
- Practise a simple review technique at home.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Explanation in simple words |
|---|---|
| Memory | The ability to remember, like the pantry where we keep what we have learned. |
| Short-term memory | What we remember for a moment, like the phone number we just heard. |
| Long-term memory | What we keep forever, like the recipe we know by heart. |
| Review | Looking again at what we studied so we do not forget it, like checking clothes before putting them away. |
| Spaced repetition | Repeating what we learned with longer and longer gaps, like watering less as the plant grows. |
| Sleeping | The night-time rest the mind uses to organise what it learned during the day. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Our memory is like a house. What we learn comes in through the door and lands in the hallway: that is short-term memory. If we leave it in the hallway, it goes with the wind, like the paper forgotten by the entrance. For what we learned to reach the bedroom, where it stays forever, something more is needed: reviewing it, using it and sleeping on it.

Has this ever happened? We hear a phone number and dial it. The next day we no longer remember it. That number was in the hallway of our memory, but it did not pass to the bedroom. Now let us think of the song we have sung a thousand times: that one lives in long-term memory, in the safest place in the house.

How does what we learn pass from the hallway to the bedroom? With three keys: attention, repetition and rest. Attention is looking carefully at what we study, without distractions, like reading a recipe carefully. Repetition is going over it several times, with gaps. Rest, especially sleep, is when the house organises everything that came in during the day.

Spaced repetition is a wonderful trick. Instead of repeating the same thing ten times in one hour, we repeat it once today, once tomorrow, once in three days and once in a week. Each repetition strengthens the path in memory, like walking a path in the field: each time we pass, the path is more clearly marked.

Let us think of bread. If we leave it in the hallway, it dries out. But if we keep it and warm it a little every morning, it stays soft. Reviewing is that "warming it a little": keeping what we learned alive with short, spaced visits instead of one big effort.

Sleep is the great ally of learning. While we sleep, the mind goes over what we have seen and decides what deserves to stay. That is why studying a little every day and sleeping well is more effective than pulling an all-nighter. Sleeping is not wasting time: it is when the house does its cleaning and puts each thing in its place.

Another key is linking the new to what we already know. If we learn that "root" in maths is like the root of a plant, which holds and feeds it, we will remember it better. Memory works through connections: the more we join the new to the old, the easier it is to remember, like sewing on a new button with thread that was already there.

Stories also help. Our memory is a great storyteller: it remembers stories better than lists. If we turn a loose fact into a little story or a funny picture, it will stay with us longer. It is like putting a colourful label on the jar in the pantry.

A home trick is to explain it out loud. When we explain to someone what we have learned, we organise it in our head and discover what we do not understand. It is like helping a neighbour with a recipe: by explaining it, we notice the steps we were missing.

It is also good to study at varied times. The mind gets bored with monotony. Changing the place, the time or the way of studying (reading, writing, speaking out loud) helps memory create several paths to the same thing. It is like reaching the same square by different streets: more paths, more secure the memory.

What about forgetting? It is normal. Forgetting is not a failure, it is part of the process: memory discards what it does not use. When we review, we tell memory: "this matters to me, keep it." Reviewing is not a punishment, it is an act of care for what we have learned.

Let us use a sewing metaphor. Learning is weaving. Each review is a stitch. A single thread comes loose easily, but many stitches together form a firm fabric that does not come undone. AI, which we will use later, can remind us when it is time to review, but we are the ones who make the stitches.

In this level we will try spaced repetition with real content: for example, three new words we want to remember, or three facts from the history of our town. We will repeat them today, tomorrow and in three days. We will see how they stay.

The key to everything is patience. Memory does not work like a photocopier that copies instantly: it works like a good harvest, which needs its time. Every review, every night of sleep, every explanation out loud are days of sun for our harvest.

By the end of this level, we will know that studying is not piling up, but weaving: with attention, with spaced repetition and with rest. With that foundation, any study becomes easier and happier.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The field path.** Marta learns three new words. Today she repeats them, tomorrow she repeats them again and in three days once more. Each review marks the path of her memory more clearly.
2. **The nap after studying.** Pedro studies in the morning and sleeps well that night. On waking, he remembers what he studied better. His brain stored the lesson while he rested.
3. **Grandmother's story.** Ana wants to remember local history dates. Instead of memorising loose numbers, she joins them with little stories from the town's elders and no longer forgets them.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Choose three new words or facts you really want to remember.
Step 2: Write them on a card or in a phone note, each one with a small explanation.
Step 3: Today, read them out loud three times and say out loud what each one is for.
Step 4: Tomorrow, without looking at the card, try to remember them. Then check how many you got right.
Step 5: Wait three days and try again. Notice how, with each review, it costs you less.
Step 6: Explain what you have learned to someone at home. By explaining, it is fixed.
Step 7: Rest easy. You know that while you sleep, your memory is doing its job.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Where does what we learn go at first? a) To short-term memory, the "hallway". b) To the bedroom. c) It is always lost.
2. What three keys move what we learn into long-term memory? a) Attention, repetition and rest. b) Haste, noise and hunger. c) None.
3. What is spaced repetition? a) Repeating a hundred times in an hour. b) Repeating with gaps: today, tomorrow, in three days. c) Never repeating.
4. What does sleep do with what we have learned? a) It erases it. b) It organises it and stores it. c) Nothing.
5. Why does explaining what we learned to another person help? a) To show off. b) Because organising it out loud helps us understand it better. c) It does not help.

Answers: 1-a, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Memory is personal: let us not pretend to know by heart what we have not yet reviewed. Honesty with ourselves is the first step.
- If someone forgets something, let us not judge: forgetting is part of the human condition and we all live through it.
- Rest is a right, not laziness: sleeping is studying, in another way.
- Let us not use tricks that cheat in exams or courses: real learning cannot be copied.
- Let us share our study techniques with other older people: teaching how to remember is caring for the community.
