## 📚 Main lesson
We have reached level twenty-five, the summit of the tree. And at the summit it is time to look at the horizon: research in education. To research is to ask questions and check them carefully. When we want to know if something works (a recipe, a method, a tool), we do not guess: we test it, measure it and compare it. That is researching, and AI accompanies us along the whole way.

Educational research asks things like: does my review method work better than the previous one? Do educational videos help my students remember more? Does large print improve understanding? Each question is a seed of research. AI helps us pose the question well and decide how to check it.

The first step of research is the question. A good research question is clear and measurable. "Do I learn better?" is not useful; "do I remember more words with spaced review than without it?" is. AI helps us turn vague questions into checkable questions, like the tailor who measures before cutting.

The second step is deciding how to measure. To know if something works, we compare the before and the after. For example: I count how many words I remember today, I apply spaced review for a month, and I count again. The difference is the result. AI helps us design the measurement: "What should I count and when?".

The third step is collecting data. The data are the answers: how many correct answers, how many days I studied, what the group said. We can keep the data in the notebook, like someone marking on the calendar each day they watered the garden. AI helps us prepare the questions for the group and organise the answers.

The fourth step is analysing. With the data in hand, we look at what they say. Did the correct answers go up? Did the group remember more with the video? AI helps us summarise the data and see the trends. It is like looking at the watering calendar at the end of the year: you see at a glance which months were good and which were not.

The fifth step is the conclusion. With the analysis done, we answer the research question: did it work or not? The conclusion is not a whim: it is born from the data. If the data say no, we learn anyway: we know what to change. Research is not about being right, it is about knowing the truth, like someone testing the soil before sowing.

AI speeds up the research work, but it does not replace it. The curiosity is ours: we choose what to ask. The observation is ours: we collect the data. The judgement is ours: we decide what they mean. AI is the calculator and the secretary, not the scientist.

There is a research that touches us very closely: we ourselves are our first subjects of study. We can research how we learn best: which schedule works for me, which technique I remember more, which days I perform best. With AI, we keep the record and discover our own learning map. It is the mirror we saw in the evaluation, but in a big way.

Educational research is also shared. When we research in community, the data of many help everyone. A club that tries a method and shares its results teaches other clubs. AI helps us bring together and share the findings. Researching together is harvesting together: each group contributes its plot.

You do not need to be a scientist to research. The everyday research, the one done with a notebook and simple questions, is worth a great deal. It is the same attitude as the grandmother who tries two bread recipes to keep the best one. Researching is asking and checking, and we all know how to ask.

Today, at the summit, we research. All that path is in our hands.

AI has been our travel companion, but the journey is ours. Every question we learned to ask, every doubt we stopped fearing, every piece of knowledge we harvested: that is what technology cannot give. The seed, the sun and the desire were ours. AI was the watering can.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Marta's research.** Marta wanted to know if spaced review worked for her. She counted the words she remembered before, reviewed for a month with cards and counted again: from 3 she went to 12. Her method worked.
2. **The club's data.** Pedro asked the club whether the educational video was understood better than the worksheet. He collected the answers and AI summarised for him: 80% preferred the video. He changed the material.
3. **Ana's mirror.** Ana researched her own schedule: she spent a week noting when she studied and how much she remembered. She discovered that in the morning she performed twice as well. She adjusted her plan to her truth.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Choose your research question: something you want to check about your learning or your group's.
Step 2: Write to AI: "I want to research this: [your question]. Help me pose it in a clear and measurable way".
Step 3: Ask: "How can I measure it? What data should I collect and when?"
Step 4: Design the measurement: what you will count before and what after.
Step 5: Collect the data over a few days or weeks, noting them down in your notebook.
Step 6: Ask AI: "Summarise my data and tell me what trend is seen". Paste the numbers to it.
Step 7: Write your conclusion: what have you discovered? What will you change?
Step 8: Write in your notebook: "I have completed Module 6. From the seed to the fruit". Congratulate yourself: you have done it.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Research with honesty: do not change the data so they give you the answer you want.
- Respect those who take part in your research: ask permission and protect their privacy.
- The results of a small research project are not laws: be humble when sharing them.
- Use the findings to improve, never to harm a person or group.
- Share what you have learned with the community: research that is kept, does not harvest.
