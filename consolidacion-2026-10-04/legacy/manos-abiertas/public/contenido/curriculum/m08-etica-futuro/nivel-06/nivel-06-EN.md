## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what algorithmic discrimination is and how it affects real people.
- Recognize that AI makes decisions in important areas: loans, insurance, housing.
- Learn which groups of people are usually left worse off.
- Know that the law prohibits discrimination, even when a machine does it.
- Develop the ability to detect when an automatic decision is unfair.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Discrimination | Treating a person differently and unfairly because of a characteristic of theirs. |
| Algorithm | The step-by-step recipe the machine follows to make a decision. |
| Automatic decision | A decision made by the machine without human involvement. |
| Unfair treatment | When someone receives worse treatment than they deserve, with no valid reason. |
| Vulnerable groups | People at greater risk of suffering injustices (older people, minorities, the poor). |
| Complaint | The act of asking for explanations or reparation when we are treated badly. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we saw that AI can have biases. Now we take a step further: when that bias is applied in real life and harms people, we talk about algorithmic discrimination. That is, the machine not only thinks in a distorted way, but also makes decisions that treat people unfairly.

Let us think about the physical world. In a shop, it would not be fair to charge a person more because of the way they speak. In the digital world, the machine can do similar things without anyone noticing. For example, an algorithm that decides who gets a loan could, without saying so, grant it more easily to some neighbourhoods than to others. And the people from the harmed neighbourhood do not even know why.

How is that possible? Because the machine learns from past data. If in the past loans were granted more to one group than to another (for historical reasons, not for ability to pay), the machine will learn to repeat that pattern. The unfair past becomes an unfair future. It is like a family recipe passed down from generation to generation, even though the recipe is wrong.

Algorithmic discrimination happens in many places. In insurance: an older person may pay more because the machine learned that older age is associated with more risk. In housing: an algorithm can filter out tenants from a neighbourhood. In hiring: the machine can discard résumés based on signals unrelated to ability. In healthcare: the machine can give less importance to the ailments of some groups than to those of others.

A very important fact: discrimination can be invisible. In face-to-face dealings, we see who treats us badly. With machines, we see nobody. The decision arrives in an email or a message, without explanation. That is why algorithmic discrimination is so dangerous: it is like a plumber who fixes the pipes badly and hides the leaks behind the wall. You cannot see them, but the dampness eventually shows.

Which groups usually end up most harmed? Those that were already vulnerable: older people, minorities, people with fewer resources, those living in disadvantaged areas. The machine does not invent inequality: it copies it from society. But by copying it at great speed and in silence, it magnifies it. What used to be one person's prejudice now becomes a rule applied to millions.

The law says discrimination is prohibited. And that prohibition also applies when a machine makes the decision. There is no "it's the system" excuse: we created the system. If a company uses an unfair algorithm, the company is responsible, even if it was the machine that "decided". It is like the driver of a car: the car is not to blame, but the driver answers.

What can we do as citizens? First, know that algorithmic discrimination exists and is not a movie. Second, detect it: if a decision seems unfair to us and they do not explain it, be suspicious. Third, complain: ask them to explain why that decision was made. Fourth, talk about it: the more it is talked about, the harder it will be to hide the injustice.

We can also ask for help. If we believe a machine has treated us unfairly (a denied loan, a more expensive insurance), we can go to consumer associations, who know how to complain. We are not defenceless: there are laws that protect us, even if sometimes they must be asked for with kind insistence.

In this level we will learn not to swallow automatic decisions as if they came from heaven. Every decision has a human person responsible. And we, with our life experience, are very good at detecting injustices. That sense of justice is a treasure that also works in the digital world.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The denied loan.** Jorge, 65, asks for a loan and is refused. His son, 40, gets the same loan at the same bank. Jorge remembers that the machine learned from old data and asks for an explanation in writing.
2. **The more expensive insurance.** Carmen compares car insurances and the price she is offered is double her daughter's. She suspects age influences it and asks them to explain the criteria.
3. **The neighbourhood complaint.** Rosa reads in the newspaper that a delivery company charged more for delivering parcels to certain neighbourhoods. Several neighbours complained and the company had to correct its algorithm.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Get the reflections notebook and a pen.
Step 2: Think of an automatic decision you have received at some point: a loan, a rate, a discount, an offer.
Step 3: Write it in the notebook and answer: did they explain it to you? Do you know why it was made?
Step 4: Ask a free AI chat: "What is algorithmic discrimination? Explain it with an example from insurance or loans". Read the answer.
Step 5: Also ask it: "What laws protect citizens against discrimination in Europe?". Keep the answer in the notebook.
Step 6: Write a list of three signs that would make you suspect an automatic decision is unfair.
Step 7: Share your signs with a relative or classmate. Every point of view adds up.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is algorithmic discrimination? a) When the machine treats groups of people unfairly with automatic decisions. b) A printer fault. c) When the AI does not understand a joke.
2. Where does the machine learn to discriminate from? a) From past data, which sometimes contains injustices. b) From its conscience. c) It does not learn, it is born knowing.
3. Is a company responsible if its algorithm discriminates? a) No, it is the machine's fault. b) Yes, the company is responsible for its algorithms. c) Only if the machine is very big.
4. Why is algorithmic discrimination dangerous? a) Because it is visible and loud. b) Because it is invisible and applies to millions of people in silence. c) Because it only affects computers.
5. What can we do if we are treated unfairly by an automatic decision? a) Nothing, resign ourselves. b) Ask for an explanation, complain and talk about it. c) Turn off the phone.

Answers: 1-a, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- The machine does not invent inequality, but it copies it and magnifies it at great speed.
- Discrimination is prohibited, even when an algorithm does it: the company is responsible.
- Automatic decisions do not come from heaven: there is always a responsible human behind them.
- If a decision seems unfair and they do not explain it, we have the right to suspect and complain.
- We older people have a sense of justice: let us use it in the digital world too.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
When bias is applied in real decisions and harms groups of people, we talk about algorithmic discrimination. It happens in loans, insurance, housing and hiring, almost always invisibly. The company is responsible, even if the machine decides, and the law protects us. In the next level we will talk about another danger with many letters: misinformation and hoaxes.
