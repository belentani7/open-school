# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 00
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 1 hour

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what ethics is with examples from everyday life: day-to-day decisions.
- Recognize that artificial intelligence makes decisions that affect people.
- Know that ethics is the way to decide what is right and what is wrong.
- Understand that machines have no conscience: responsibility is human.
- Lose the fear of talking about difficult topics like this one, calmly and respectfully.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Ethics | The set of principles that help us decide between right and wrong. |
| Decision | The choice we make when there are several possible options. |
| Artificial intelligence | Machines or programs that learn from data and do tasks that people used to do. |
| Responsibility | The commitment to answer for our actions and their consequences. |
| Conscience | The ability to feel, understand and value what we do. |
| Dignity | The value that every person has simply for being a person. |

## 📚 Main lesson
When someone gives us a plant, we learn to water it, give it light and prune it. That care is a form of responsibility: we know the plant depends on us. With technology something similar happens. The machines we use every day are neither good nor bad by themselves: they depend on how we use them. And on who creates them. That way of deciding what is right and what is wrong is called ethics.

Let us think of a shared kitchen. In that kitchen, knives are used to cut vegetables but can also cause harm. Nobody blames the knife when someone uses it badly. The blame, or the credit, belongs to the person who uses it. The same happens with artificial intelligence: it is a very useful tool, like a good kitchen knife, but someone must decide how it is used and with what limits.

Artificial intelligence is already among us. When the phone suggests words as we type, when an app recommends a movie, when the bank decides whether to grant us a loan, there is artificial intelligence behind it. Many of those decisions affect real people. That is why we need to think about ethics: so that those decisions are fair and respect people.

The word ethics comes from a Greek word meaning "character" or "custom". In life, we all practice ethics every day, even if we do not call it that. When we decide to return a lost wallet, when we do not lie to a friend, when we help an older neighbour, we are deciding what is right. Ethics is not a distant subject: it is what we do every day.

With technology, ethics becomes more important because machines have no conscience. A computer program does not feel, does not suffer and does not value. It can learn from data and repeat patterns, but it will never understand whether something is fair. That is why, when a machine makes a decision, a human being must make sure that decision respects people. That task belongs to all of us.

Good news: we all have experience in ethics. Anyone who has raised children, worked in a team or taken care of someone knows what it means to decide between what is fair and what is convenient. That life wisdom can be applied to the digital world. You do not need to be an engineer to have an opinion on whether an automatic decision is fair. In fact, the older we are, the more life we carry and the more we know about fairness.

Another important idea: ethics is not always having the perfect answer. Sometimes there are two options and neither is perfect. Ethics is, above all, the courage to ask the questions: is this fair? Who benefits? Who is harmed? Do I respect people by doing this? Asking is already an ethical act.

In this module we will walk a path. We will start talking about our most personal things: our data, our passwords, our privacy. Then we will talk about bigger problems: biases, misinformation, the laws that protect our rights. And at the end, we will talk about very deep things: the future of humanity and the role of every citizen.

All of this may seem overwhelming, and that is normal. These are big topics. But we will move forward slowly, like someone planting a garden: first the soil, then the seed, then the watering, and at the end the harvest. Each level of this module will be a small step, with real-life examples and no technical jargon.

There is a phrase worth remembering always: technology is a tool, not a judge. The tool does not decide whether a person deserves respect: we decide that. The more we understand technology, the better we can use it with fairness. And the more we talk about these topics, the better protected the people we care about will be.

In this first level, we will only plant the seed of the question. We do not need perfect answers. We need to start looking at the digital world with eyes of fairness, like someone entering a new house and noticing how the furniture is arranged. Little by little we will see what is right and what can be improved.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The kitchen knife.** María uses a cooking app that suggests recipes based on her history. One day the app recommends sweets and María understands that the app does not know she has diabetes: it just repeats patterns. The decision of what to cook is hers.
2. **The bank and the loan.** Jorge asks the bank for a loan and a machine decides whether to grant it. Jorge wonders: who ensures that machine treats everyone equally? That question is ethical.
3. **The phone recommendation.** Carmen shares a phone with her daughter and the phone suggests movies based on what each of them watches. Carmen discovers that the machine observes her habits and wonders what else it observes.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Get paper and a pen.
Step 2: Write a difficult decision you have made in your life (for example, changing jobs, helping someone or saying no).
Step 3: Write two words below: "right" and "wrong". Think about how you knew what was correct.
Step 4: Now think about your phone: what decisions does it make for you every day? (suggestions when typing, alerts, recommendations).
Step 5: Write a question you would like to answer about machines and people (for example: "who is in charge when the machine decides?").
Step 6: Keep the paper. We will come back to it at the end of the module to see how much we have learned.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is ethics? a) A type of food. b) The set of principles that help us decide between right and wrong. c) A computer program.
2. Can a machine know if a decision is fair? a) Yes, always. b) No, machines have no conscience. c) Only big machines.
3. Who is responsible when a machine makes an unfair decision? a) The machine. b) Nobody. c) The people who create and use it.
4. Do you need to be an engineer to have an opinion on the fairness of an automatic decision? a) Yes. b) No, life has taught us a lot about fairness. c) Only if you work at a bank.
5. Is asking questions about right and wrong an ethical act? a) Yes, asking is the beginning of thinking. b) No, only the answers count. c) Only if the questions are difficult.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-c, 4-b, 5-a.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Machines do not decide alone: behind every automatic decision there are responsible people.
- We all have experience with fairness from our lives; that experience matters in the digital world.
- Asking uncomfortable questions (is it fair? who is harmed?) is the first step of ethics.
- Technology is a tool, not a judge: we decide whether people are respected.
- Talking about these topics calmly, without fear and without blaming anyone is the best way to move forward.
