# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 08
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Root
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Learn simple habits to protect data in daily life.
- Know what to do with suspicious emails and messages from strangers.
- Learn the concepts of phishing and identity theft.
- Understand how to protect the phone and computer the way we protect the house.
- Create a daily digital care routine without becoming paranoid.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Phishing | A scam that imitates a trusted company to steal our data. |
| Impersonation | Pretending to be another person to deceive. |
| Link | An internet address hidden behind a text or button. |
| Update | A fix that the app or the system installs to correct faults. |
| Backup | A copy of our data stored elsewhere in case something fails. |
| Double-checking | Verifying information through another channel before trusting. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In previous levels we learned what biases and hoaxes are. Now comes a very practical part: how to protect our data in daily life. It is like maintaining the house: you do not need to be a builder to know you have to close the door, check the lock and not open the door to strangers.

Let us start with email. We all receive digital letters, and some are lies. Phishing is a very common scam: a message that imitates a bank, a delivery company or an official office, and asks us for data "for security" or "to fix a problem". A real bank never asks for our password by email or message. If an email asks for it, it is phishing.

How to recognize it? Several signs. The first: urgency. Phishing messages are always in a hurry: "your account will be closed in 24 hours", "reply now". The second: the threat or the incredible offer: "you have won a prize", "there is a pending charge". The third: the mistakes: spelling errors, strange addresses, senders we do not know. It is like detecting a false door: the handle does not match the lock.

Another trick of scammers is the fake link. An email may contain a button that says "Click here" but that, inside, leads to a page imitating the bank's. If we enter and type our data, we hand it over to the scammer. To protect ourselves, it is better not to enter important pages from emails: we open the browser and type the address ourselves, like walking to the bank office along the known street.

Impersonation is when someone pretends to be us. With our data (name, photo, documents) a scammer could open accounts or ask our contacts for money. That is why we never send photos of documents by message, neither to strangers nor even to "companies" that ask for them out of the blue. It is like handing over a copy of the house key: it is only given to whoever truly needs it.

Protecting the phone and the computer is another routine. Two very simple gestures: do not leave the door open (lock the screen with a password or fingerprint) and keep the house fixed (install the updates when asked). Updates are not a whim: they are repairs that cover holes through which hackers could enter. Pressing "update now" is like fixing the leak before the roof breaks.

The backup is another golden habit. It means keeping a copy of our photos and documents somewhere else: in the cloud or on an external drive. If we lose the phone or it breaks, we do not lose everything. It is like keeping a second copy of grandma's recipe in the drawer: if the first one gets wet, the second one is still there.

You should also distrust strangers kindly. A message from someone we do not know who introduces themselves as "a distant relative with a money problem" is a classic scam, especially against older people. Before doing anything, call a trusted relative and tell them about the message. The double-check by phone is a precious defence.

A daily digital care routine does not need to be long. It can be: in the morning, review messages without rushing and do not open the suspicious ones. At night, log out of accounts if we use a shared computer. And once a week, look at the phone's notifications and updates. They are small gestures that, together, build a wall.

It is not about living in fear. It is about living with open eyes, just as we walk down the street: we are not afraid, but we look before crossing. Our digital life deserves the same care as our physical life.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The "bank" email.** George receives an email from the "bank" asking for his key "due to a security problem". The sender's address is strange and there are mistakes. He clicks nothing, calls his bank and they confirm it is a fraud.
2. **The "grandson" call.** Carmen receives a call from someone claiming to be her grandson, with an urgent problem and asking for money. Carmen is frightened, but applies the rule: she calls her daughter and confirms the grandson is fine.
3. **Rosa's backup.** Rosa saves the grandchildren's photos in the cloud following the class advice. When her phone breaks, she recovers all the photos without losing anything.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open your email on the phone or computer.
Step 2: Find an email from a company or service (bank, shop, delivery) and open it.
Step 3: Look at the sender: does the address end in @bank.com or is it a strange address?
Step 4: Hover the mouse over any link (without clicking it) and look in the corner at the destination address: does it match the company?
Step 5: Ask yourself: does it ask me for personal data? Does it have urgency or a threat? Write down the signs in your notebook.
Step 6: Look in the phone for the "Backup" option and check if your photos are being saved in the cloud. If not, activate it by following the steps on the screen.
Step 7: Write in your notebook your three personal rules of daily protection.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is phishing? a) A fish dish. b) A scam that imitates a trusted company to steal our data. c) A type of email.
2. Does a real bank ask for your password by email? a) Yes, sometimes. b) No, never. c) Only if there is a problem.
3. What do we do before entering a bank's page from an email? a) Click the link without looking. b) Better not to enter from the email: open the browser and type the address ourselves. c) Send the email to a friend.
4. What are updates? a) Fixes that cover security holes. b) Advertising. c) Messages from the company.
5. What is the backup for? a) To keep a copy of our data in case we lose the phone. b) To make paper copies. c) To send photos to the family.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-a, 5-a.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- If we receive a scam, let us not be ashamed: scammers are very skilful, and reporting it helps others.
- Never hand over personal data to whoever asks for it in a hurry or with threats.
- If someone tries to impersonate you, act: report it and warn your contacts to protect them.
- Protecting our data is also protecting the family, which can be dragged into a scam.
- Do not forward fraud messages to others: cut the chain.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Protecting our data is caring for the digital house: not opening doors to strangers, not entering pages from emails, keeping updates up to date and keeping backups. Phishing and impersonation are defeated with calm, double-checking and common sense. In the next level we close the Root band with a review and a personal defence plan.
