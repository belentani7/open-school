# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 1 hour

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a password is and why it is the key to our accounts.
- Learn to create strong passwords that are easy to remember.
- Learn what two-step verification is and how to turn it on.
- Know why we should not reuse the same password everywhere.
- Take the first steps to protect the phone, email and accounts.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Password | A secret word that opens an account, like the key to a door. |
| Account | A personal space inside an app or service (email, bank, social network). |
| Two-step verification | A second check that asks, in addition to the password, for a code from the phone. |
| Hacker | A person who tries to enter someone else's accounts or systems without permission. |
| Code | A secret number sent to the phone to confirm that it is us. |
| Password manager | An app that stores all our passwords under a single key. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Imagine that every door in our house had the same key. If someone got a copy, they could walk into the kitchen, the bedroom and the storage room. It is exactly the same with passwords. Every account is a door: email, bank, social networks. Using the same password for everything is like having a single key for the whole house.

A password is a secret word that opens an account. It is the digital key. It must be ours, secret and hard for others to guess. We should not share it with anyone, not even family members, because whoever has the password can enter the account and pretend to be us.

What makes a password strong? First, that it is long: the longer it is, the harder it is to guess. Second, that it is not predictable. Avoid your partner's name, your date of birth, "123456" or "password". Those details are easy to guess because they are on our networks or in documents. It is like putting a lock with the key hidden under the doormat: everyone looks there.

A good technique for creating strong passwords is to use a long phrase that only we know. For example, "MyGrannySewsAtNoon". It is long, easy to remember and hard to guess. Or combine three unrelated words: "RoseBicycleRain". You remember it like a little story and it is very hard to figure out.

We should not write our passwords on a paper stuck to the computer or in a phone message. If the paper or the phone falls into the wrong hands, so do the passwords. A better option is a password manager: an app that keeps all the keys under a single master password. It is like a digital key ring: one key to open the ring and all the others inside.

Two-step verification is another great protection. It works like this: in addition to the password, the service asks us for a code that arrives by text message on the phone. It is like having two locks: even if someone steals the key to the first one, they cannot open it without the second. It costs a little more to get in, but it also costs hackers a lot more.

Many older people hesitate to enable two-step verification because it seems complicated. In fact, it is very simple: once we activate it, every now and then the phone will ask us for a code we receive by message. It is an extra step that protects us enormously. It is worth learning, just as we learned to pay by card.

It is also important to have a different password for the email. Email is the master key: with email, almost everything else's password can be recovered. If the email falls into the wrong hands, almost all accounts are in danger. That is why the email password must be the strongest of all and must not be reused anywhere else.

Sometimes apps warn us: "your password has appeared in a data breach". That means someone published a list of stolen passwords. If we get that alert, we must change the password as soon as possible. It is like being told the lock has been forced: we do not wait, we change the door.

In this level we will put into practice one small but very valuable thing: review our passwords, improve the weak ones and enable two-step verification on the most important accounts. You do not have to do it all in one day. Just start with the email, which is the master key.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The email key.** Rosa used the same password ("rosa1958") for everything. Her daughter helped her change the email one to a long phrase and enable two-step verification.
2. **The school message.** Antonio receives a message claiming to be from his bank asking for his password "for security". He remembers the rule: no serious bank asks for passwords by message. He does not reply.
3. **The confirmation code.** Carmen enables two-step verification on WhatsApp. Now, every time she uses a new phone, she receives a code and knows that only she can open her account.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Get your phone and a notebook to write down your goals.
Step 2: Write in the notebook the list of your most important accounts: email, bank, WhatsApp, social networks.
Step 3: Choose a long phrase that only you know to create your new password (for example, the title of a song from your youth combined with a number you remember).
Step 4: Change your email password. Look in the email for the "Security" or "Password" section and follow it.
Step 5: Enable two-step verification on your email: look for "Two-step verification" and press "Turn on". Choose the text-message code option.
Step 6: Repeat step 5 with WhatsApp if you have it installed: Settings, Account, Two-step verification.
Step 7: Write in the notebook what you changed today and what you will change next week.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a password? a) A secret word that opens an account. b) A type of phone. c) A profile picture.
2. Why should we not reuse the same password on all accounts? a) Because it is boring. b) Because whoever steals one will be able to enter all of them. c) Because it wears out.
3. Which is a strong password? a) "123456". b) "MyGrannySewsAtNoon". c) "rosa1958".
4. What is two-step verification? a) A second check that asks for a code in addition to the password. b) Changing the password twice. c) Setting two identical passwords.
5. Does a serious bank ask for your password by message? a) Yes, always. b) Sometimes. c) No, no serious bank asks for passwords by message.

Answers: 1-a, 2-b, 3-b, 4-a, 5-c.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Our passwords are keys to our digital life: protecting them is protecting our dignity.
- Sharing passwords with others can also put the people we care about at risk.
- Never give your password to anyone who asks for it by phone or message, even if they claim to be trustworthy.
- Using someone else's password to enter their account is a violation of their privacy.
- Enabling two-step verification is an act of care for ourselves and our family.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Passwords are the keys to our accounts, and each account should have a different, strong key. Two-step verification adds a second lock and multiplies protection. Email is the master key and deserves the strongest password. In the next level we will discover what artificial intelligence shares when we talk to it.
