# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 17
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Branch
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what digital identity is: who we are on the internet.
- Learn the concept of digital reputation: the impression we leave on others.
- Know that what we publish can last a long time and reach very far.
- Learn to take care of our own image and that of others.
- Discover how a good digital reputation is built.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Digital identity | The set of data, photos and traces that represent us on the internet. |
| Digital reputation | What other people think of us based on what they see on the internet. |
| Digital footprint | Everything we leave published or consulted in the digital world. |
| Digital erasure | The right to ask for content that harms us to be removed. |
| Profile | The image others build of us with what they see. |
| Image privacy | Control over who uses our photos and our face. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the neighbourhood, people know us by how we are: by our word, by how we treat the neighbours, by our way of being. On the internet something similar happens, but with a difference: the footprint we leave can be more lasting and bigger than a handshake. Our digital identity is who we are in the digital world, and our digital reputation is how others see us.

Digital identity is made of many pieces: our photos, our messages, the websites where we are, the comments we leave, even the searches we make. It is like a portrait made of crumbs: everything we do on the internet is a crumb, and together they form the image others see. We do not always choose it: sometimes other people upload photos of us or talk about us.

Digital reputation is the opinion others form. A good digital neighbour has a reputation for trustworthiness: they keep their word, do not lie, do not spread hoaxes, respect people. A bad digital neighbour leaves a reputation that follows them. And the most important thing: digital reputation can precede us. A future employer, a bank or an acquaintance can search our name on the internet before dealing with us.

How does "searching our name" work? It is a very widespread habit: typing a person's name into a search engine and seeing what comes up. If something negative comes up (a compromising photo, an aggressive comment, a fake news story), the first impression changes. That is why taking care of what is seen of us is not vanity: it is prudence. It is like keeping the house door and the facade in order.

What is published on the internet lasts a long time. An impulsive message, a photo that seems funny to us today, a bad-tempered comment: all of that can stay stored for years, and can appear in places we do not imagine. It is like ink on a cloth: once it falls, it is very hard to remove. That is why the golden rule is to think before publishing: would I like this to be seen in ten years' time?

But not everything is negative. Our digital reputation can also be built with good actions: sharing useful things, helping others, leaving respectful comments, showing our knowledge. Whoever publishes with generosity and respect builds a facade they can be proud of. Digital reputation is like a garden: you sow it, you care for it and it bears fruit.

And what about photos? Our face is ours. Nobody should publish our photos without permission, nor should we publish others'. Asking permission before uploading someone's photo is a basic rule of respect. And it also applies to the family: that funny photo of the sleeping grandson may not please the parents. Asking is always the best policy.

If there is already something on the internet that harms us and we did not put it there, we have resources. The right to digital erasure allows us to ask for content that harms us to be removed (we saw this in the Stem band with the right to be forgotten). It is not always easy and not everything is always achieved, but it can be tried: sending the request, asking the data protection authority for help. We are not defenceless.

We also have to learn to read what we see of others. A person can have an unfair reputation because someone spread a hoax about them. That is why the honest digital citizen does not share rumours or destroy reputations. Before thinking badly of someone because of what we see on the internet, let us remember that appearances can lie and that a photo out of context does not tell the whole story.

In this level we have learned to look after our digital facade. Identity and reputation are not only a celebrity thing: they belong to everyone. Every post, every photo, every comment is a crumb of the portrait others see. Caring for it with respect, thinking before publishing and asking permission, is a sign of digital maturity.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Rosa's search.** Rosa types her name into the search engine and finds an old party photo she does not like. She asks for it to be removed and learns to control better what is published about her.
2. **Antonio's permission.** Antonio takes a funny photo of a friend and, before publishing it, asks him. The friend says no, and Antonio respects it. Trust is worth more than a photo.
3. **Carmen's garden.** Carmen shares useful tips in her gardening club and respects everyone. Little by little, her name on the internet becomes associated with good people and good advice.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Take your notebook and write: "My digital identity and reputation".
Step 2: Open the browser and search your first and last name in the search engine. Look at what comes up.
Step 3: Write down what you found: are there photos? Comments? Is it information that represents you well?
Step 4: Do the golden rule test: think of your last post (photo, message). Would you like it to be seen in ten years' time?
Step 5: Review your social networks (if you have them) and check whether there are photos you prefer not to show. Decide whether to remove them.
Step 6: Ask an AI chat: "How does an older person take care of their digital reputation?". Read the answer and write down two tips.
Step 7: Reflect aloud: what would you like people to find when they search your name?

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is digital identity? a) The set of data, photos and traces that represent us on the internet. b) The identity card. c) The email password.
2. What is digital reputation? a) What others think of us according to what they see on the internet. b) A social media award. c) The connection speed.
3. Can what we publish on the internet last a long time? a) No, it erases itself. b) Yes, it can stay stored for years and reach very far. c) Only if a company publishes it.
4. What do we do before publishing someone else's photo? a) Publish it quickly. b) Ask permission. c) Publish it only if it is funny.
5. What is digital erasure? a) The right to ask for content that harms us to be removed. b) Clearing the browser history. c) Deleting contacts.

Answers: 1-a, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-a.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Our face and our image belong to us: nobody should publish them without permission.
- Thinking before publishing is a sign of respect towards ourselves and towards others.
- Let us not spread rumours or destroy anyone's reputation: appearances lie.
- If something harms us on the internet, we have the right to ask for its erasure.
- A good digital reputation is built with respect, like a well-tended garden.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Our digital identity is the portrait we leave with photos, messages and traces, and our reputation is how others see us. What is published can last a long time and reach far, so we think before publishing and ask permission for others' photos. If something harms us, we have the right to erasure. In the next level we will talk about the broadest personal defence: cybersecurity.
