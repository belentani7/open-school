# Module 8: Ethics, Future and Governance — Level 10
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what the GDPR is, the European law that protects our personal data.
- Learn the rights that law gives us: to know, to rectify, to erase.
- Know that companies must ask permission and explain what they do with the data.
- Learn that we have the right to ask and to complain.
- Discover the data protection authority, our ally.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| GDPR | The European law that protects people's personal data. |
| Right of access | The right to know what data they hold about us and what they do with it. |
| Right of rectification | The right to correct data that is wrong. |
| Right of erasure | The right to ask that our data be deleted ("right to be forgotten"). |
| Data protection authority | The public body that monitors compliance with the law. |
| Transfer | The sending of our data to another company or country. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We enter the Stem band. So far, protection depended on our habits. Now we discover something comforting: there are written laws that protect us, made for people like us. The most important one in Europe is the GDPR, whose initials mean General Data Protection Regulation. It is the law that looks after our information.

Let us think about the market. If a seller wanted to write down everything we buy, who we are and where we live, there would be rules preventing it without our permission. The GDPR does that in the digital world: companies cannot treat our data as they please. They have to ask permission, explain what they use it for and answer to the law if they do not.

A key idea of the GDPR is consent. When a website asks us "Do you accept cookies?", it is not a boring formality: it is the law forcing them to ask our permission. Companies must explain, in clear words and not in fine print, what they want our data for. And we can say yes or no, without being thrown out of the site.

The GDPR gives us concrete rights, like keys to our own house. The right of access lets us ask: "what data do you hold about me and what do you do with it?". The right of rectification lets us correct: "my surname is misspelled, fix it". The right of erasure lets us ask: "delete my data", which is called the right to be forgotten.

All those rights sound very good, but how are they exercised? It is simpler than it seems. To ask for access, erasure or rectification, we write to the company (they usually have a section called "data subjects' rights" or "data protection") and ask for what we want. The company must respond within a reasonable time. You do not need to be a lawyer: writing clearly and politely is enough.

And if the company ignores us? Then we have a second resource: the data protection authority. In Spain it is called the Spanish Data Protection Agency. It is the public body that makes sure the law is complied with. We can file a complaint, and they investigate. It is like the square's guardian: if a neighbour breaks the rules, the guardian acts.

The GDPR also obliges companies to look after our data: not to keep it longer than necessary, to protect it with security measures and to warn us if there is a leak. If a company loses our data, it cannot hide it: it must notify us. It is like a bakery: if a bug fell into the flour, it could not keep selling without warning.

An important clarification: the GDPR protects people, not companies. If a shop wants to know another shop's data, the GDPR does not cover them the same way. But you and me, as people, it covers us always, no matter where we live in Europe or which company processes our data. It is a law written with people in mind.

What does artificial intelligence have to do with all this? A lot. AIs feed on data, sometimes personal data. The GDPR requires that, if a company uses our data to train an AI, it does so with permission and explaining it. Moreover, when a machine makes an important decision about us, the GDPR gives us the right to have a person intervene and to receive explanations. We will see more of this in the coming levels.

In this level we have planted the stem of the law. Like the stem that rises towards the light, our laws rise towards justice. And now we know that, when it comes to our data, we are not alone: there is a regulation that defends us and an authority that listens to us.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **Rosa's question.** Rosa asks an online shop: "what data do you hold about me?". The shop has a data protection section and replies with a list. Rosa exercises her right of access.
2. **The misspelled surname.** Carmen discovers that a company has her surname wrong. She writes a brief rectification request and, within a few weeks, it is corrected.
3. **Antonio's complaint.** A company does not delete his data when he asks. Antonio files a complaint with the Spanish Data Protection Agency and the company has to comply.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Open the browser and search for "Spanish Data Protection Agency". Enter its website (aepd.es).
Step 2: Find the "Citizen" or "Your rights" section and open it.
Step 3: Read the descriptions of the rights: access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability.
Step 4: Write in your notebook, in your own words, what the right of access and the right of erasure mean.
Step 5: Think of a company you have given your data to. Write a sentence with which you would ask it to know what it does with them.
Step 6: Look on the Agency's website for the button or section to "file a complaint" and observe how the process is, without sending anything yet.
Step 7: Write a reminder in your notebook: "If a company does not respect my data, I can complain to the Data Protection Authority".

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Companies must ask permission clearly and explain what they use our data for.
- Having written rights is not enough: you have to exercise them, and exercising them is an act of dignity.
- Complaining politely and firmly also protects others, because it forces the company to comply.
- Fine print must not hide anything: transparency is an ethical and legal obligation.
- Our data always belongs to us, even if we said "yes" long ago: we can change our mind.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
The GDPR is the European law that protects our personal data and gives us rights: to know, to correct, to erase and to complain. Companies must ask permission clearly and respond to our requests. If they do not comply, the data protection authority is our ally. In the next level we will discover a more recent law, designed for artificial intelligence: the European AI Act.
