# Module 2: Tools Without Code — Level 01
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what a web browser is and why it is the door to the internet.
- Recognise the parts of a browser: address bar, buttons and tabs.
- Learn to type an address correctly and to move forwards and backwards.
- Use a search engine to find information with simple words.
- Save your favourite pages so you never lose them.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|------|--------------------|
| Browser | The programme that lets you see web pages, like the window of a house that shows you the whole street. |
| Address bar | The long box at the top of the browser where you write where you want to go. |
| Tab | Each open window inside the browser, like several tables in a restaurant, one for each dish. |
| Search engine | A page that looks for things on the internet for you, like a helpful librarian. |
| Link | A word or image that takes you to another page when you click on it, like a door in a corridor. |
| Bookmark | A saved page so you can return to it without looking for it again, like a bookmark in a book. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Imagine that the internet is a huge city. To visit that city you need a vehicle, and that vehicle is the browser. The browser is a programme that lives on your phone or computer and lets you see the pages that exist on the internet. Without it, the internet exists, but you cannot see it, just as a city exists even if you do not go there.

You probably already know the most common browsers by name, even if you do not know they are called that. Chrome, Edge, Safari, Firefox. Each is a different brand of the same tool, like different makes of car that all take you to the same city. The browser is usually the icon with a circle of colour, or a fox, or a compass. Do not worry about which one to use: they all do practically the same thing.

The browser has a few essential parts, and once you know them, you know them all. The first is the address bar: it is the long, empty box at the top. It is where you write the name of the page you want to visit. Writing an address is like giving a taxi driver the name of a street. If you write it correctly, you arrive; if you write it wrongly, you end up somewhere else.

The second essential part is the buttons to go back and forward. When you are on a page and you want to return to the previous one, you press the arrow that points left. It is like returning to the room you have just left. The browser remembers the trail of your steps, like a trail of breadcrumbs.

The third part is the tabs. A tab is an open window inside the browser. If you open several tabs, you can have several pages open at the same time without closing any of them. It is like having several tables in a restaurant: on one table the starter, on another the main course, and you move from one to the other as you wish.

Now, the most important skill: how to find something on the internet without knowing its address. For that there is the search engine. The best known is Google, but there are others, such as Bing or DuckDuckGo. The search engine is a page where you write what you are looking for in normal words, and it returns a list of pages that talk about it.

Searching is easier than it seems. You do not need to write with perfect grammar. If you want the weather in your town, write "weather in [your town]". If you want the timetable of the doctor's surgery, write "doctor's surgery timetable". The search engine understands your words and offers you the best answers, like a shop assistant who guides you to the shelf you need.

A word of caution: not everything you see in the results is the best. The first results are often advertisements. Learn to look at the address of the page before clicking. Official pages usually have a recognised name or end in .gov, .edu, or the name of a company you trust. If in doubt, ask a trusted person.

When you find a page that you like and want to visit often, save it. That is called adding a bookmark. In the browser there is a star or a symbol with that purpose. Pressing it saves the page so you can return later with one click. It is the same as putting a bookmark in a recipe book so you do not have to look for the page again.

One last thing: the browser can be your friend for life, but it also needs care. Update it when it asks you to, because updates repair problems and make it safer. And never accept strange warnings that tell you to install something "urgently": they are like the people who stop you on the street offering things. In the ethical section we will see this better.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The bus timetable**: you want to know at what time the bus to the centre passes. You open the browser, write "bus timetable [your town]" in the address bar and press enter. The page appears and you save it as a bookmark.
2. **Reading the news**: your brother tells you about a piece of news. You open the browser, write the name of the newspaper in the search engine and click on the first official result. You read the news and close the tab.
3. **The doctor's appointment**: you have been told you can ask for an appointment on the internet. You search for "health centre [your neighbourhood] appointment", find the official page and follow the steps. Now you know how to do it for the whole family.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
1. Open the browser on your phone or computer.
2. Look at the parts: find the address bar at the top.
3. Write "wikipedia.org" in the address bar and press enter.
4. On the Wikipedia page, search for your town and click on it. You have just visited a real page.
5. Press the back arrow and then the forward arrow. Watch how the browser remembers your steps.
6. Open a second tab and search for the weather in your town.
7. Click on a link you like and then come back with the back arrow.
8. Save the Wikipedia page in the bookmarks, using the star or the menu symbol.
9. Close all the tabs and open the browser again. Find the bookmark you saved.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a browser and what is it for?
2. What is the address bar and what do you do in it?
3. What is a search engine and what is it used for?
4. How do you go back to a page you have already visited?
5. What is a bookmark and why is it useful?

**Answers:** 1. The programme that lets you see web pages; it is the door to the internet. 2. The long box at the top where you write the address of the page you want to visit. 3. A page that finds information on the internet from your words, like a helpful librarian. 4. Pressing the back arrow. 5. A saved page so you can return with one click, without searching again.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
The browser sees where you go and what you look at. That information is worth a lot to companies that want to sell you things. Use private mode for sensitive searches, such as health or bank matters, and check what the browser's settings say about your data. Also be careful with pages that "congratulate you" with prizes or urgent warnings: official pages never act that way. If something seems too good to be true, it usually is.

## 🔓 Open tools
- **Mozilla Firefox** (mozilla.org) — a free and open-source browser that respects your privacy.
- **Brave** (brave.com) — a free browser that blocks advertising and trackers.
- **DuckDuckGo** (duckduckgo.com) — a search engine that does not follow your searches.
- **Bing** (bing.com) — Microsoft's free search engine.
- **Startpage** (startpage.com) — a search engine that gives you Google results without tracking you.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
The browser is the vehicle that takes you through the internet. With the address bar you write where you want to go, with the arrows you go back and forth, and with the search engine you find things without knowing their address. Save your favourite pages and take care of your safety. In the next level we will learn to create accounts and use passwords, the key that opens the doors of your digital home.
