# Module 2: Tools Without Code — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what an account is and why so many tools ask you to create one.
- Learn what a good password is and how to invent one you can remember.
- Know the difference between a password and a passphrase.
- Recognise the two-step verification as a second lock on your door.
- Learn to manage your accounts without fear and without writing passwords in unsafe places.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|------|--------------------|
| Account | A space of your own inside an application, with your name and a key that is your password. |
| Password | A secret word that opens your account, like the key to your house. |
| Passphrase | A password made of several words joined together, longer and easier to remember. |
| Two-step verification | A second lock: apart from the password, a code arrives on your phone to prove it is you. |
| Username | The name or email you use to identify yourself, like your name written on the mailbox. |
| Password manager | A trusted drawer that keeps all your passwords locked and opens with a single master key. |

## 📚 Main lesson
When an application asks you to create an account, what it is really asking you is: "open your own little house here". That little house has your name on the door and a lock. The lock is your password. Inside, the application keeps your things: your messages, your photos, your appointments. That is why it is so important to take care of the key.

Why do so many tools ask for an account? Because they need to know who you are and distinguish you from other people. It is like a residential building: the postman needs to know that a letter is for you and not for the neighbour. The account is your name on the letterbox. And just as you do not want anyone to open your letterbox, you do not want anyone to open your account.

The first rule of a good password is that it is long. A short key opens in seconds. A long key takes years. Think of it like a bike lock: a small padlock cuts with one snip; a thick chain resists. Modern tools measure your password and tell you if it is strong or weak. Listen to them, they are your friends.

The second rule: do not use personal things that everyone can guess, such as your birthday, your pet's name or your football team. That information is public on social networks or known by your family. It is like leaving the key under the doormat: everyone knows where to look.

The third rule: do not reuse the same password for everything. If someone finds the key to the letterbox, they should not be able to open your bank with the same key. Each account, its own key. I know it seems like a lot, but for that there is the password manager, which we will see in a moment.

Now, the trick to inventing strong and memorable passwords: the passphrase. Instead of a word, use four words that make a small scene. For example: "blue horse summer kitchen". It is long, it has no relation to you, and you remember it because it is a picture. The machines cannot guess it; you can. Write it the first time in a safe place and you will see how quickly you memorise it.

Two-step verification is like having a second lock. When you enter your password, the application sends a code to your phone, and without that code you cannot get in. Even if someone stole your password, they could not get in without your phone. Turn it on everywhere they offer it to you, especially in the bank and in email.

Where should you write your passwords? Not in a notebook in clear and not in a document on the computer. The safest place is the password manager: a programme that keeps all your keys locked and opens with a single master key. Some are free, such as Bitwarden. The master key is the only one you must remember.

A word about usernames: your username is usually your email or a name. It is not secret, it is your name on the letterbox. What is secret is the key, the password. Never share it with anyone, not even with someone who says they are from "technical support". A real company never asks you for your password.

Beware of the great trap: messages that say "your account is in danger, click here". They are like someone ringing your doorbell to say they have a problem with your lock and asking you to give them the key. Real companies address you by name and never send you links in a hurry. When in doubt, close the message and go to the official page yourself.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The video call**: your daughter wants to video call you, and the application asks you to create an account. You choose your email, a passphrase you have invented, and activate two-step verification. Now you have your own corner to talk to the family.
2. **The online shop**: you want to buy a gift. You create an account with a new, different password. The application offers you to save the password: you accept with the password manager. You never have to remember it again.
3. **The council website**: to ask for a certificate, the page asks for an account. You create it calmly, with a strong passphrase, and you save it in the manager. The next time, it opens with one click.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
1. Choose an application you already use that has an account, or use the bank's if you have one.
2. Look for the password settings in the menu (usually "settings" or "security").
3. Invent a passphrase of four words that draw a small picture. Write it in a provisional paper.
4. Change your password to that passphrase if the application allows it.
5. Look for the two-step verification option and activate it. Watch how a code arrives on your phone.
6. Open a password manager, for example Bitwarden, and create your account with a strong master key.
7. Save the passphrase in the manager. Then try to close and reopen with the manager.
8. Destroy the provisional paper: now the key lives in the manager.
9. Write in your notebook the list of accounts you have, without passwords.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is an account and what is its key called?
2. What are the three basic rules for a good password?
3. What is a passphrase and why is it easier to remember?
4. What is two-step verification for?
5. Where is the safest place to keep your passwords?

**Answers:** 1. It is your own space inside an application, and its key is called the password. 2. It must be long, must not contain personal information that can be guessed, and must not be reused for different accounts. 3. A password made of several words joined together that forms a mental picture, easier to remember and harder to guess. 4. To add a second lock: a code on your phone that proves it is you. 5. In a password manager, which keeps them locked and opens with a single master key.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Your passwords are yours and no one else's. Never share them, not even with family "for convenience". Remember that it is not you who is the product: many "free" tools live on the data they collect about you. Read what they ask for when creating the account and think about whether it is reasonable. And if someone steals your password, act fast: change it, tell the bank if relevant, and learn from the situation without shame. We all make mistakes; the wise person fixes them quickly.

## 🔓 Open tools
- **Bitwarden** (bitwarden.com) — a free and open-source password manager.
- **KeePassXC** (keepassxc.org) — a free password manager that lives only on your computer.
- **Proton Pass** (proton.me) — a free password manager focused on privacy.
- **Have I Been Pwned** (haveibeenpwned.com) — a page to check if your email has appeared in a data leak.
- **Two-Factor Auth** (2fa.directory) — a list of services that offer two-step verification.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
An account is your own little house in every application, and the password is its key. Use long passphrases, do not repeat keys and open the second lock of two-step verification. Keep everything in a password manager. In the next level we will meet the chatbots, ChatGPT and Gemini, and we will learn to talk to them as if they were a helpful assistant.
