## 📚 Main lesson
Today we open the Stem band, and we do it with a surprise: we are going to run programmes without knowing how to program. How is that possible? Thanks to Google Colab, a free workspace from Google that lives in the browser. To understand it, imagine a restaurant kitchen where someone else has already prepared everything: you just have to press the right button. That is Colab.

A "notebook" is a document that mixes two things: text and programme parts. It is like a recipe book that explains, step by step, and next to each explanation has the ingredients and the steps ready. In Colab, each of those parts is called a cell. A cell can be text, which you read, or a piece of programme, which you execute. And the beauty is that the programme cells are already written.

Why is this so important for you? Because thousands of people, organisations and universities have published ready-made notebooks for everyday tasks. Translate audio, convert documents, analyse a list, generate images, create tables. You do not write the programme: you open the notebook, press the play button of each cell, and the machine does the work. The kitchen is already cooked; you just serve it.

Let us look at the parts of Colab. When you open a notebook, you see a series of boxes, the cells. Some have a play button on the left, a triangle: those are programme cells. To run them, you press that triangle. You will see how, after a moment, a result appears below. It is like pressing the start button of a washing machine: you put the clothes, press, and it does the cycle.

There is a very common fear: "and if I break something?" It is practically impossible. Colab is a temporary workspace: if you make a mistake, you close the tab and open it again. Nothing is damaged, nothing explodes. The worst that can happen is that a cell gives an error, and then you ask for help or close and start over. No one has broken anything by pressing a button.

A golden rule for today: run the cells in order. The notebook cells follow a sequence, like the chapters of a book. If you run the third before the first, the programme may not have what it needs. Start at the top and run one by one, from top to bottom. It is like following a recipe: you do not put the cake in the oven before having the mixture.

Now, how do you get to Colab? The easiest way is through your Google account. Search for "Google Colab" in the browser, or go to colab.research.google.com. There you will see a welcome screen with options: open a notebook, or see ready-made examples. You do not need to install anything, because everything lives in your browser, and your files are saved in your Google Drive, like a folder in the cloud.

The most valuable skill: using notebooks that others have prepared. In the "Examples" or "Github" section of Colab you will find thousands. And in the next levels we will see where to find them. For today, practise with one of the examples and understand the flow: text that explains, cells that run, results that appear. You do not have to understand the programme; you have to know how to run it.

And a final idea, very liberating: you are not here to learn to program, but to learn to use what others have programmed. Just as you do not build your car but you know how to drive it, you do not write the programmes but you know how to run them. That is the philosophy of the Stem band: use the power without carrying the weight.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The text transcription**: a niece sends you an audio of a meeting. You open a ready-made notebook for transcription, run the cells, and in minutes you have the written text. You have used a programme without writing a single line.
2. **The data table**: the association wants to organise a list of members. You open a notebook that reads and sorts tables, press play, and it returns the list ordered and clean. Ready to print.
3. **The document converter**: they send you a file in a format you cannot open. You open a notebook that converts formats, run it, and you have the document in the format you need.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
1. Open your browser and go to colab.research.google.com. If it asks you, sign in with your Google account.
2. Look at the welcome screen: find the button "New notebook" and click on it.
3. Look at the first cell: it says "Untitled0.ipynb" at the top, and below there is a cell.
4. Change the cell to text: in the cell type, choose "Text", and write: "My first notebook. Today I am going to run a programme."
5. Add a code cell: press the "+ Code" button.
6. In the code cell, copy this: print("Hello, this is my first programme")
7. Press the play button (the triangle) of that cell. Look at what appears below.
8. Run the cells of a ready-made example: look for the "Examples" section and open one.
9. Write in your notebook: what happened when you pressed play, and how you feel now about running programmes.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is Google Colab and what is it for?
2. What is a notebook and what does it mix?
3. What is a cell and what is it for?
4. Why is it important to run the cells in order?
5. Can you break something in Colab?

**Answers:** 1. A free Google workspace that runs programmes in your browser, without installing anything. 2. A document that mixes text, which explains, and programme parts, which execute. 3. Each box of the notebook; there are text cells and programme cells. 4. Because the cells follow a sequence, and if you skip steps the programme may lack what it needs. 5. Practically not: it is a temporary workspace, you can close and start over without breaking anything.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Running a notebook means running a programme that someone else has written. That programme can do almost anything on your Google account, because Colab works with your credentials. Do not run notebooks from unknown people without thinking: it is like letting a stranger cook in your kitchen. If the notebook asks you for personal data or permissions that seem excessive, stop and ask. Running is an act of trust; choose whom you trust.
