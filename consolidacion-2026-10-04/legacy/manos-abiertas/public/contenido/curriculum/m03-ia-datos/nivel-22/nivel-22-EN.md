# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 22
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Canopy
## Estimated time: 3 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Analyse a clean table with AI tools without programming.
- Write clear questions in everyday language and understand the answers.
- Verify the numbers the AI gives before trusting them.
- Tell apart a pattern described by the AI from a cause explained by us.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Natural language | Talking to the tool the way we talk to each other, without codes. |
| Prompt | The question or instruction we write to the AI. |
| Analysis | The stage of the cycle (Level 13) in which we look for patterns and answers. |
| Automatic summary | The list of findings that the AI extracts from the table for us. |
| Verification | Checking by hand the important numbers the AI gives us. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In Level 21 we left the table clean and tidy. Today the most exciting moment arrives: asking the AI about our data, without writing a single line of code. Because analysing data is no longer a job only for specialists in white coats with code: it is a conversation. You show your table to an AI tool and ask it questions the way you would ask a knowledgeable friend.

The key is that the AI understands everyday language. It does not need strange commands: it needs a clean table and a clear question. It is like asking in a shop: if you know what you want and you say it calmly, the answer is good. If you ask "what is best for me?" without context, any answer is of little use. Context is half of the question.

There are two ways to analyse with AI. The first: smart spreadsheets, which offer analysis with one click (summaries, averages, the most notable data). The second, more powerful: upload the file to an AI chat and talk to it. You can upload your sheet as a file or paste the table directly. Then you ask whatever you want to know and the AI answers by looking at your data.

How do you write a good request? With three ingredients: context, task and format. The context says what the table is: "this table is my spending for one month". The task says what we want: "which category do I spend the most on?". The format says how we want the answer: "give me the three biggest expenses with their amounts". With these three, the answer is almost always useful.

Take Rosa's budget as an example. She uploads her table and writes: "This table is my spending this month. What do I spend the most on and how much should I set aside for essentials?". The AI answers with the category, the amount and sometimes a chart. The question that Rosa found hard to answer by looking at forty receipts, the AI summarises in seconds. But watch out: that is where the work starts, not where it ends.

The AI also does the numbers: sums, averages, comparisons. But the AI can be wrong, and sometimes it is wrong with total confidence. That is why verification exists: checking by hand at least one of the important numbers. If the AI tells you your biggest expense is 312 euros, open the sheet, add up that category and confirm it. Five minutes of checking prevent a year of false conclusions.

Another danger: the AI describes patterns, but it does not know why they happen. If the AI sees that your electricity bill goes up in winter, that is a pattern. That it goes up because it is cold and the heating is used more, that is a cause that you explain with your knowledge. Let us remember Level 10: correlation is not causation. The machine shows you the what; the why is human work.

And the ethics of Level 19 accompanies us here. If the AI sees a relationship between two things, do not turn it into a judgement about people. An AI that sees that a neighbourhood spends less on health says nothing about its residents: it says something about the data. The machine inherits the bias of its data. You, with your judgement, are the one who sets the limits.

In our final project, the AI analysis answers the question we asked in Level 20. The AI is the assistant that does the heavy work of looking at the figures; you are the one who decides what it means and what to do. This division of tasks —the machine calculates, the person decides— is what separates good use of AI from bad use.

For an immigrant, analysing your own data is a tool of empowerment. Knowing how much goes to rent, food and money sent home allows better planning. Comparing prices, preparing a complaint with figures, or showing the SEPE or the bank clear and organised data: all of this is real power, and now it is in your hands.

In Level 23 we will learn to draw the findings: to turn the numbers the AI helped us understand into clear and honest charts that anyone can understand. Because a well-analysed piece of data deserves to be well told. For now, enjoy the magical moment of this level: asking your data questions and receiving answers.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Marta's shopping
Marta uploads her month's shopping list to an AI chat and asks: "how much do I spend per week and what do I spend the most on?". The AI tells her fruit is the biggest expense. Marta checks the total in the sheet and confirms it. From then on she decides to buy fruit at the market.

### Example 2: Pedro's residents' community
Pedro uploads the community's expenses table and asks why the water bill is going up. The AI shows him the pattern: the last three months rise more. Pedro calls the company and asks for the detailed bills; he is the one who discovers the cause.

### Example 3: Carmen's town temperatures
Carmen uploads the open data from Level 14 and asks the AI: "compare the summers of 20 years ago with the ones now". The AI describes the change. Carmen verifies the numbers with an average in the sheet and writes her conclusion carefully.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open your clean sheet from Level 21 ("My clean data").
Step 2. Think of a concrete question that your table can answer.
Step 3. Upload the file to an AI chat (Gemini or similar) or paste the table.
Step 4. Write your question with the three ingredients: context, task and format.
Step 5. Read the answer and ask for a summary with the three main findings.
Step 6. Verify one number: calculate in the sheet with SUM or AVERAGE and compare with the AI.
Step 7. Ask "what patterns do you see?" and write down which ones are correlations (Level 10) and not causes.
Step 8. Save the answer in your project and write down what you have learned from your data.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What do you need to analyse data with AI? a) To know how to program. b) A clean table and knowing how to ask clear questions. c) A very expensive computer.
2. How do you write a good request to the AI? a) With context, task and format. b) In a single word. c) With lots of technical jargon.
3. What should you do with the numbers the AI gives you? a) Always believe them. b) Verify at least one by hand. c) Ignore them.
4. If the AI sees that you spend more on heating in winter... a) That is the cause: it is cold. b) It is a pattern; you explain the cause. c) It is a lie.
5. Who decides with the data? a) The AI. b) You, with the AI as your assistant. c) The program that created the AI.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Verify before sharing: a wrong figure from the AI spread through WhatsApp can harm many people.
- Do not invent: if the AI does not know something, let it say so; do not fill in what you do not know with guesses.
- Other people's data: do not upload other people's data to an AI tool without permission (Level 19).
- Correlation is not causation: describing a pattern is not explaining the cause; be honest in your conclusions (Level 10).
- Transparency: note in your project that an AI did the analysis and that you reviewed it.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- To analyse with AI you do not program: you have a conversation with a clean table.
- A clear request has three ingredients: context, task and format.
- The AI can be wrong: always verify the important numbers.
- The AI describes patterns; you explain the causes and make the decisions.
In Level 23 we will learn to draw the data and to read charts with judgement, so that we can tell well what we have discovered.
