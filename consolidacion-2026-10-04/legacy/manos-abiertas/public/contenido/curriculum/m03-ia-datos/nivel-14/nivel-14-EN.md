## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Plain-language explanation |
|---|---|
| Open data | Public data that anyone can download, use and share for free. |
| Data portal | A website where open data is published and downloaded. |
| CSV | Simple table format: comma-separated values. |
| Licence | The conditions that say how data can be used. |
| Metadata | The information that describes the data: what it measures, when, from where. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we saw the data life cycle and learned that every project begins with collection. But there is a problem: we do not always have the data. What do we do if we want to know how the temperature in our region has risen over the last 30 years, or how many births there are in our country? The answer is a jewel of our time: open data.

Open data is data that governments, international organisations and other bodies publish on the internet so that anyone can download and use it free of charge. It is data "of everyone, for everyone". It is not a favour: many countries have laws that oblige administrations to publish the data they collect with public money. What we pay for with our taxes also belongs to us as data.

Where do you find it? There are data portals at every level. The European Union has "data.europa.eu", which holds the data of its 27 countries. Spain has "datos.gob.es"; Portugal, "dados.gov.pt". Worldwide, organisations such as the World Health Organization, the World Bank and NASA publish free data: diseases, economics, climate, space. If you are interested in a topic, there is almost certainly a portal with real data about it.

Open data arrives in many formats, but there is one that interests us especially because it is the "lingua franca" of tables: CSV. CSV stands for "comma-separated values". It is a very simple text file where each line is a row and commas separate the columns. Although it may look inelegant, CSV is the most universal format that exists: any program, from any era, opens it.

How do you open a CSV? In Google Sheets, for example: "File", "Import" and upload the file; or drag it onto the browser window. You can also open it directly with a double click if you have a spreadsheet installed. If the columns come out split wrongly, don't panic: it is just a separator problem. During import you must say whether columns are separated by comma, semicolon or tab, and choose the right one.

Before trusting a dataset, look at its "metadata": the record that describes the data. Serious portals always include it. What does this data measure? Which years does it cover? Who collected it and with what method? Is it updated? Data without metadata is suspect. Metadata is like a food label: without it, we do not know what we are eating.

Another thing to check is the licence. Open data usually has free licences that allow use, but each one has its conditions: some ask you to cite the source, others forbid selling the data, others ask for nothing. Most portals explain this in a corner of the page. Using data without respecting its licence is like using someone else's photo without permission: it can be done, but it is unfair and sometimes illegal.

Here we have a fantastic opportunity for learning: open data is real data, not invented. In level 11 we cleaned a sheet we made up ourselves. Now we can download real data, with real errors, about the temperature of our city, and put the whole cycle into practice: download, clean, analyse, visualise and decide. Nothing teaches more than working with data that truly matters.

And here is a secret for the future: AI is trained on open data. The large datasets that universities and companies use to train artificial intelligences are largely published free on these portals. When in the Branch band we learn to make predictions, open data will be our raw material. What you learn to download today, you will use tomorrow to feed your first AI.

With this level we finish the Stem band. We have learned to distrust false correlations, to clean data, to build dashboards, to plan projects and to find real data. The plant now has a firm stem. In the next band, the Branch, comes the most exciting part: making data guess the future. Predictive analysis is just around the corner, and you reach it with the ground prepared.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: The temperature of your city
Download from a national portal the temperature data for your capital over the last 10 years. Open the CSV in Google Sheets and draw a line. You see the seasonal rise and, perhaps, an underlying trend.

### Example 2: Births in your country
Download the birth rate data for the last 20 years. Use a formula column to calculate the year-by-year change and discover when the birth rate started to fall.

### Example 3: The council budget
Search the municipal portal for spending by area. Sort the column from largest to smallest and discover which area takes the biggest share of the budget. That gives you material to ask about at the next neighbourhood meeting.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open your browser and go to the open data portal of your country or region (for example, datos.gob.es or your region's portal).
Step 2. Search for "monthly average temperature" or a topic that interests you, such as "rainfall" or "population".
Step 3. Choose a dataset and download the CSV file (if there are several formats, choose CSV).
Step 4. Open the metadata record and read: what does it measure, which years, who publishes it. Write down two sentences.
Step 5. Open the CSV in Google Sheets: "File", "Import" and choose the file.
Step 6. If the columns come out wrong, repeat the import and change the separator (comma, semicolon or tab).
Step 7. Do a quick cleaning review (level 11): are there empty cells or impossible values?
Step 8. Draw a line chart with the variable that interests you (for example, the average temperature by year).
Step 9. Write below the chart a sentence with what you see: "July temperatures have risen 1.5 degrees in 10 years".
Step 10. Save the sheet with the name "Open data" and note the source and download date. Congratulations: you have worked with real data.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Open data is a great gift of democracy: knowledge financed with public money and given back to citizens. Using it well is an honour: cite it, respect its licence and do not distort it. And remember that behind every figure there are real people: a hospitalisation figure is not a number, it is someone who was ill. The responsibility of people who work with open data is to return something useful and truthful to the common good.
