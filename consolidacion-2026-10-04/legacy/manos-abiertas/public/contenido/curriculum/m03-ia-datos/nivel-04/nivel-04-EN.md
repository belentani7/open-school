## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Chart | A drawing that represents data so you can understand it at a glance. |
| Bar chart | Bars compared with each other; each bar is one value. |
| Line chart | A line joining the values over time, showing the trend. |
| Pie chart | A circle cut into slices; each slice is a part of the whole. |
| Axis | The ruler that measures the chart: the horizontal and vertical axes. |

## 📚 Main lesson
There is a famous saying: "a chart is worth more than a thousand words". And it is true. Look at a table of fifty numbers: understanding it takes time and effort. Draw those same numbers in a bar chart and, within three seconds, the brain has already seen the most important things: which bar is the tallest, which is the shortest, and how they compare. The chart does not invent anything: it just presents the same data in a way the eyes understand better.

Why do the eyes understand a chart better? Because the human brain is built to process shapes, colours and heights, not columns of numbers. When we see bars, the eye compares heights effortlessly. When we see a line going up, we immediately understand that something is growing. The chart uses the sense of sight, which is very fast, instead of slow counting.

There are three basic charts worth knowing the way you know a knife, a fork and a spoon: each one serves a different dish. The bar chart compares amounts: the fruit of the week, spending by month, sales by shop. Each bar is one value, and the bars are compared with one another. The line chart shows how something changes over time: the week's temperature, the year's electricity use. The pie chart (the "cake") shows the parts of a whole: what the budget is spent on, what the shopping basket is made of.

How do you choose the right chart? Ask yourself a question before drawing: do I want to compare things, see a change over time, or see the parts of a total? If I am comparing shops, bars. If I am looking at electricity use over a year, lines. If I want to see what percentage of spending is fruit, pie. Choosing the right chart is like choosing the right tool: you do not hammer a nail with a screwdriver.

Today we are going to create our first chart in Google Sheets, from the "My week's expenses" table in the previous level. The process is very simple: we select the data, press "Insert" and "Chart", and the sheet draws one by itself. Then we choose the chart type in the panel that appears on the right. The spreadsheet does the drawing; we only decide what we want to see.

A tip for getting the chart right: select the header together with the data. If you select "product" and "price" including the titles, the sheet knows what to put on each axis and puts understandable labels. If you select only numbers, the chart appears, but without names, and nobody will know what each bar means. Titles are the bread on the table: the chart is understood thanks to them.

The axes are the rulers of the chart. The horizontal axis (bottom) usually holds the categories: products, months, names. The vertical axis (left) holds the values: prices, amounts. When the chart shows "product on the bottom axis, price on the left", it is saying: each bar is a product and its height is its price. Reading the axes is the key to reading any chart.

Now let us read other people's charts. Open a newspaper or a web story and find a chart. Before looking at the bars, look first at the axis titles: what does each axis measure? Then look at the tallest and shortest bars: what do they represent? Finally, ask yourself: is the chart being honest? Sometimes axes start at a number other than zero, and that makes differences look bigger than they really are. Reading charts with a critical eye is a citizen superpower.

Let us go back to our table. With the same data, we can draw three charts and tell three stories. With bars, we see which purchase is the most expensive. With lines, we would see how spending rises and falls across the days of the week. With pie, we see what proportion of the total each category is. The table is the same; the story changes with the question and the chart chosen. That is thinking like an analyst: not just looking at data, but deciding which question you want to answer.

A warning about pie charts: they should only be used when the slices add up to a whole. The total shopping basket, the family budget, the result of a vote. If there is no reference "whole", slices confuse. And you should not use more than five or six slices, because very small ones cannot be told apart. To compare many things, bars; for parts of a whole, pie.

In this module, the chart is our "bridge" between data and understanding. The data we sorted and classified (levels 2 and 3) become drawings that tell stories. And later we will see that artificial intelligence also "looks" at data and summarises it in patterns, in a way similar to how we summarise with a chart. But let us not run ahead: today we learn to draw and read our first charts.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: The week's spending in bars
With the "My week's expenses" table, create a bar chart with "product" on the horizontal axis and "price" on the vertical. At a glance you will see which was the most expensive purchase.

### Example 2: Electricity use in lines
Write down in a table your last 6 electricity bills: month and consumption. Draw a line chart. The line will show you whether your use goes up or down over the year.

### Example 3: The shopping basket in pie
In your expenses table, classify the purchases by category (fruit, bread, cleaning) and add up each one. Draw a pie chart: you will see what part of the basket each group occupies.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open https://sheets.google.com and open the "My week's expenses" sheet.
Step 2. Make sure the table has a header and at least 5 rows with data.
Step 3. Select with the mouse the "product" and "price" columns, including the titles.
Step 4. Click the "Insert" menu and choose "Chart".
Step 5. A panel will appear on the right and the sheet will draw a chart automatically (probably bars).
Step 6. In the panel, under "Chart type", confirm it is "Column chart" (vertical bars).
Step 7. Look at the chart: each product is a bar and the height is its price. Which is the tallest?
Step 8. Close the panel with the X. The chart is embedded in the sheet. Click on it and drag a corner to make it bigger.
Step 9. Click on the chart title (if it appears) and give it a clear name, for example "Week's expenses".
Step 10. Save (in Google Sheets it saves automatically). Now you have your data turned into a picture you understand at a glance.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Why does a chart help you understand data better than a table?
2. Which chart is right for seeing how something changes over time?
3. Which chart shows the parts of a whole?
4. What should you look at first when reading someone else's chart?
5. Why do you need to select the header together with the data when creating a chart?

Answers: 1. Because the brain processes shapes and heights faster than columns of numbers. 2. The line chart. 3. The pie chart. 4. The axis titles, to know what each one measures, and then the most striking bars or points. 5. So that the sheet puts understandable labels on the axes and the chart has meaning.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
A chart can tell the truth or lie. If the axis starts at a number other than zero, the differences look bigger. If data that are not convenient are cut off, the story changes. When you create charts, present them honestly: without cutting the axes or hiding numbers. And when you read other people's charts (news, adverts, politicians), always ask yourself: what axis are they using? Is some data missing? A critical chart reader is harder to deceive.
