# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 00
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what data are, with examples from everyday life.
- Recognize that data are everywhere: receipts, lists, contacts, photos.
- Tell the difference between a single piece of data and useful information.
- Start "seeing" the data around you every day.
- Take the first step towards a personal data notebook.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Data (singular: datum) | A single fact, like a number, a name or a date. On its own, it says very little. |
| Information | Data brought together and ordered so that they tell something useful. |
| List | A collection of data placed one after another. |
| Record | A row with data about the same thing or person. |
| Raw material | The basic material used to make something. Data are the raw material of AI. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Think of a cooking recipe. A recipe has two parts: the ingredients and the steps. If you only have the ingredients on the counter, there is still no dish. You need the recipe that tells you what to do with each ingredient. Well, data are the ingredients of modern technology. Artificial intelligence, the programs on your phone and websites are all made with data, just as a stew is made with vegetables.

A piece of data is a single fact. For example: "3", "Mary", "12 March", "45 euros". On its own, a piece of data tells you almost nothing. "3" could be three apples, three euros or three o'clock in the afternoon. Data gain meaning when we put them together with other data and arrange them. When we gather data and arrange them well, we get information.

Look around you. How many data can you see in a single minute? The price of a kilo of tomatoes at the market is data. The phone number you have saved in your address book is data. The shopping list in your pocket is a collection of data. The recipe for baked rice that your mother passed on to you is data too: amounts, times and temperatures are all data.

Think about the contacts list on your phone. Each contact is a record: a name, a number, sometimes a photo. Together they form a list of data. When you search for "Mary", the phone sorts the data and finds her in an instant. Without the data, the phone would not know who Mary is or how to call her.

Another example: your electricity bills. Each bill contains data: the date, the kilowatt-hours you used, the price per kilowatt-hour and the total to pay. If you keep your bills for the last year and look at them all together, you discover when you use more and when you use less. Those bills, which look like boring papers, are actually very valuable data.

The photos on your phone are data too. Each photo stores millions of tiny dots of colour. When artificial intelligence recognises your grandson in a photo, what it does is examine those dots and compare them with photos it has already seen. That is why we say AI "learns from data": without data, there would be no AI.

Now comes the most important idea in this level: a single piece of data is not worth much on its own. It is like one single piece of a jigsaw puzzle. Information appears when the pieces fit together. For example: you see the data "15 euros" on a receipt. What does that tell you? Nothing, by itself. But if you combine that data with "Saturday shopping basket" and with "3 kilos of fruit", then the data already tells a story: roughly how much fruit costs that Saturday.

To organise data you do not need a computer. The grandmother in our story did it with a paper diary and a pencil. She wrote down the household expenses every day and, at the end of the month, she added them up. That diary was a small paper database. Today we can do the same, but with tools that save us the work of adding and sorting.

Think of the wardrobe in your home. If the clothes are piled up, looking for a blue jumper takes a while. But if the clothes are folded and sorted by season and by colour, finding it is a matter of seconds. Sorting data works the same way: the very same pile of data, well ordered, becomes a useful tool.

In this module, called "AI Applied to Data", we will learn to handle data the way a cook handles ingredients. First we will learn to recognise them, then to sort them, then to draw them in charts and, much later, to let artificial intelligence analyse them for us.

Do not worry if this sounds big. We are not going to program or use strange words. We will work with things you already know: shopping lists, receipts, contacts and calendars. Only now with a new way of looking: the look of someone who knows that those papers and lists hide valuable information.

For this level you only need two things: a notebook or a phone to take notes, and curiosity. Nothing else. In the next level we will look closely at the data of our everyday life: the receipts, lists and contacts we already have at hand.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: The shopping list
This week, write your shopping list with the price of each product. At the end, look at the numbers: you will be able to say how much you spend on fruit, bread or cleaning products. That is already using data.

### Example 2: Grandmother's diary
Your mother or grandmother wrote down expenses in a diary. Each entry is a piece of data. At the end of the month, she added them up. You can do the same with the notes app on your phone: each purchase is a line of data.

### Example 3: The photos on your phone
Open the gallery on your phone and look at how it is organised: by date, by place. Each photo is a piece of data and the phone has sorted them all on its own. These are the data that AI uses to recognise faces and places.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Take a notebook or open the notes app on your phone.
Step 2. Write the title: "My data of the week".
Step 3. Write today's date at the top of the page.
Step 4. Make a list of 5 things you bought this week and their price. Example: "Bread: 2 euros".
Step 5. Below, write 3 data that are not money: a time, a place or a name.
Step 6. Read the page out loud. Each line is a piece of data. Together, they form information about your week.
Step 7. Keep the page. We will use it in the next level to sort and classify.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Data tell things about our lives: what we buy, when, how much we spend. That information is ours. Sharing a single piece of data seems harmless, but many data together can reveal a great deal about us. That is why, in this level, we start with a golden rule: personal data are cared for the way you care for the keys to your home. Do not share them with strangers and always ask who you are giving them to and what for.
