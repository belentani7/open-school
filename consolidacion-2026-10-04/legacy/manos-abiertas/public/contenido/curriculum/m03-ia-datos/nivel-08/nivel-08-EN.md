## 🎯 Level objective
- Master the line chart for seeing changes over time.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Trend | The general direction of a line: up, down or flat. |
| Peak | A point much higher than its neighbours, like a mountain. |
| Dip | A point much lower than its neighbours, like a valley. |
| Pie chart | The "cake": a circle cut into slices that add up to 100 %. |
| Slice | Each piece of the pie; its size shows its part of the whole. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In level 7 we conquered the bars: the king of comparison. Today we complete the court with two essential servants: the line and the pie. Each answers a different question, and using them well is a matter of knowing which question you have in your hand. The line answers "how does this change over time?" and the pie answers "what part of a whole is each thing?".

Let us start with the line. A line chart joins points in time order: January, February, March... Each point is a value of that moment, and the line joining them shows the story. If the line goes up, the thing is growing; if it goes down, it is shrinking; if it stays flat, it is steady. The message is not in a single point, but in the direction of the line: the trend.

How do you read a line with good judgement? First look at the whole picture, not the first point. Is the line rising, falling or steady overall? Then look for the peaks: the highest points, those mountains that catch the eye. What happened that month? Christmas, summer, sales? Then look for the dips: the valleys. A peak and a dip next to a cause reported in the news is the basis of all interpretation of data over time.

An everyday example: electricity use. If we draw a line with the last 12 months, we will see it rise in winter (heating, more hours of lights on) and fall in summer. That line tells the story of your home better than any single bill. And if one year the line rises much more than the previous one, something has changed at home: a new appliance, more time at home, a colder winter.

The line has a golden rule: it must not be used with categories that are not time. Comparing ice cream flavours with a line makes no sense; the line only means something when the points are ordered by time (or by another natural progression, like age). If there is no time order, use bars. The line is the chart of time, as the barometer is the instrument of the weather.

Now to the pie. The pie chart divides a circle into slices; each slice is a part of the whole. It answers: "how is the whole divided?". What the family budget is spent on, what the shopping basket is made of, how the votes are distributed. The essential condition: the slices must add up to a complete whole (100 %).

The pie has three rules. First: do not use more than five or six slices; small slices cannot be told apart. Second: order the slices from largest to smallest, starting at 12 o'clock; that way it reads like a clock. Third: if the differences are small (48 % against 52 %), the pie disguises them; for fine differences use bars. The pie is for big, simple messages.

An example of good pie use: the family shopping basket. If in a month you spent 200 euros, and 90 went on fruit, 60 on bread, 30 on cleaning and 20 on pharmacy, the pie shows at a glance that fruit takes almost half of the pie. That message, in a table of numbers, would be hard to read; in a pie, you see it instantly. But careful: "almost half" is easy to see, "exactly 47.3 %" is not; for precision, the table rules.

And when should you avoid the pie completely? When there are many categories, when the differences are small, or when the whole is not clear. And a special case: three-dimensional slices or "doughnut effect" are dangerous decoration; they distort the angles and deceive the eye. The flat, simple pie is the only honest one.

With bar, line and pie we complete the basic visual language. The rule for choosing is a question: am I comparing things? Bar. Does it change over time? Line. Parts of a whole? Pie. If you can answer that question before drawing, your charts will always tell the right story. And if someone shows you a strange chart, you now have the words to ask: what type is it and what question does it answer?

This trio of charts is also the gateway to the statistics of level 9. Bars and pies summarise; statistics summarises with numbers: the mean, the median and the mode. Charts are the drawing of the summary; statistics, its figures. Together they form the analyst's complete tool. In level 9 we will join the drawing and the figure.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Electricity use in a line
Write down in a table the consumption of the last 6-12 months and draw a line chart. Identify the trend (up, down or steady), the peak and the dip, and tell what happened in those months.

### Example 2: The shopping basket in a pie
With your month's expenses table, add up by category and draw a pie. Check that the slices add up to the total and read which one dominates.

### Example 3: Choosing the right chart
Take three questions: "which shop sells more?", "how are my savings evolving?", "how is the budget divided?". Assign each one its chart and explain it out loud.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open Google Sheets and create a new sheet called "My electricity use".
Step 2. Write in A1 "month" and in B1 "consumption". Fill 6 rows with months and approximate consumption (example: January 300, February 320...).
Step 3. Select the two columns with the header and click "Insert" and "Chart".
Step 4. In the panel, choose "Line chart".
Step 5. Look at the line: is it rising, falling or steady? Point with your finger at the peak and the valley.
Step 6. Go back to the "My week's expenses" sheet and create a pivot table by category (review level 6 if you need to).
Step 7. Select the pivot table and create a second chart. This time choose "Pie chart".
Step 8. Look at the pie: is there a tiny slice? If so, group it with another or into "others".
Step 9. Order the slices from largest to smallest if needed (under "Customise", "Pie" tab).
Step 10. Compare the two charts: the line tells a story over time; the pie tells how a total is divided. Write a sentence with each story and share it with a family member.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What question does the line chart answer?
2. What is the trend of a line and how do you recognise it?
3. What is the essential condition for using a pie chart?
4. Why should you not use a line to compare ice cream flavours?
5. When is it best to avoid the pie chart?

Answers: 1. "How does this change over time?". 2. It is the general direction of the line: up, down or flat; you recognise it by looking at the whole picture, not a single point. 3. The slices must add up to a complete whole (100 %). 4. Because the line only makes sense with a time order or natural progression; without it, use bars. 5. With many categories, small differences, or when the whole is not clear; and always avoid the 3D or doughnut effect.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
The line and pie charts can also deceive. A line can start "cut" at a high value to exaggerate a rise. A three-dimensional pie distorts the angles and makes a small slice look bigger. When you present these charts, use full axes and flat pies. When you read them, ask yourself what is being emphasised and what is being hidden. Seeing is not believing: seeing is checking.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- The line shows changes over time; you read it by its trend, peaks and dips.
- The pie shows parts of a whole; it requires the slices to add up to 100 %.
- To choose: compare = bar, time = line, whole = pie.
- Beware of cut axes in lines and 3D pies.
In the next level basic statistics arrives: the mean, the median and the mode, the numbers that summarise an entire table in a single figure.
