# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 01
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Recognise the data we already have at home: receipts, lists, contacts and calendars.
- Learn to read an electricity or water bill like someone reading a treasure map.
- Discover that every row of data has "boxes" with names (fields).
- Understand what each piece of data they ask for on forms is for.
- Start collecting your own data in an orderly, clean way.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Field | Each box or category in a record, such as "name", "date" or "price". |
| Row | A complete line of data, all about the same thing. |
| Column | All the data of the same type, one below the other. |
| Form | A sheet with empty boxes that ask us to fill them in. |
| Consumption | The amount of something we use, such as electricity or water. |

## 📚 Main lesson
In the previous level we discovered that data are everywhere. Now we will take out the magnifying glass and look closely at the data we already have at home, without buying anything. Pick up your latest electricity bill, from the drawer or the bedside table. That paper, which we normally throw away, is a little goldmine of data.

Look at the bill calmly. You will see it has boxes: at the top your name and address; in the middle the date the meter was read; further down the kilowatt-hours used this month; and at the end the total to pay. Each of those boxes is a "field". A field is a category of data, as if it were a drawer in a filing cabinet. "Date", "consumption" and "price" are fields. The value of each field is the actual data: "12 March", "230 kilowatt-hours", "45 euros".

Now think about the contacts list on your phone. Each contact is a row: a line with all the data about the same person. Inside each row there are fields: first name, surname, phone, address, email. The phone keeps all the rows together, and each row has the same fields as the others. That is why the phone can sort contacts by surname or find "Mary" among thousands of names: because the data are arranged in rows and columns.

This business of rows and columns is so important that it will stay with you throughout the whole module. Imagine a sheet of lined notebook paper. Each line is a row. If on each line we write "product, price, date", we get a table. The first line, the one with the titles, is called the header and it says what is in each column. All the other lines are data. That is how a spreadsheet works, and that is how the biggest databases in the world work.

The data of our lives also appear in forms. When the bank asks you for your name, ID number and phone number, what it is doing is filling in fields of a record. When a website asks you for your email and a password, it creates a new row in its database. Every form is a door: in front of it we decide what data we give and what we do not. Nobody can force you to give more data than is necessary.

And what is all this for? Think about the month's shopping. If you write down every expense with its "category" field (fruit, bread, cleaning, pharmacy), at the end of the month you can answer questions like: how much do I spend at the pharmacy? Did the bread purchases increase compared with last month? Where does the money go? Without fields and order, the piles of receipts answer nothing. With fields and order, they answer everything.

Doctors use fields too. The medical record has fields like age, blood pressure, medication and allergies. When the doctor asks "are you allergic to anything?", they fill in a field. Those fields, arranged over the years, are what allow the doctor to see how your health is evolving. Well-organised medical information can save lives.

Another everyday example: the shopping list. Most of us write it on a loose piece of paper. But if we turn it into a table with three columns — "product", "quantity", "price" — it is already a small data record. And if we keep the table every week, after a month we have enough data to know what we always buy and what we forget.

Now an important warning: data are only worth anything if they are clean and honest. A receipt written with the wrong date, a wrongly copied price, or a contact with a half-finished number are dirty data. Dirty data deceive. In this module we will learn to look after our data the way we look after the pantry: no expired items, no wrong labels.

You do not need any program to start. With a sheet of paper, a ruler and a pencil you can create your first data table. Draw four columns: "date", "product", "quantity" and "price". Every purchase you make is a new row. That paper table will be the basis of today's activity and of the next levels, where we will move everything into a spreadsheet on the computer.

The central idea of this level is simple but powerful: our receipts, lists and contacts are not old papers; they are data with fields, rows and columns. Whoever learns to see them that way is already thinking like a data analyst. In the next level we will take one more step: we will learn to sort and classify that data so that it begins to tell stories.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Reading an electricity bill
Take a bill and write down five fields: date, consumption in kilowatt-hours, price per kilowatt-hour, total and taxes. Now you have a row of data about your home. Do the same with the bills from the last three months and compare the consumption.

### Example 2: The contacts on your phone
Open the contacts list and choose three people. For each one, write down the fields the phone keeps: name, phone and email. You will see that all contacts share the same fields.

### Example 3: A shopping list with columns
Instead of a loose piece of paper, draw three columns on a sheet: "product", "quantity", "price". Each purchase is a row. At the end of the week, add up the price column and you will know exactly how much you spent.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Take a sheet of paper and a ruler.
Step 2. Draw a table with four columns: "date", "product", "quantity" and "price".
Step 3. In the first row (the header), write the names of the columns.
Step 4. Look at your kitchen or pantry and choose 5 products you would buy this week.
Step 5. Write one row per product, filling in the four fields. Example: "Monday, tomatoes, 2 kilos, 3 euros".
Step 6. Below the last row, draw a line and write "Total" in the price box.
Step 7. Use a calculator to add up the five prices and write the total.
Step 8. Keep the sheet. In the next level we will learn to sort and classify this data.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is a field? Give an example from an electricity bill.
2. What is the difference between a row and a column?
3. Why can the phone find a contact so quickly?
4. What are "dirty data"? Give an example.
5. What questions can a shopping list with columns answer?

Answers: 1. A box or category of data; for example, "date", "consumption" or "total to pay". 2. A row is all the data about the same thing; a column is all the data of the same type. 3. Because contacts are stored in rows with the same fields, and that allows sorting and searching. 4. Data with errors or gaps; for example, a wrong date or a wrongly copied price. 5. How much I spent, on what, and what I always buy or forget.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Every form we fill in asks for data, but not all of it is necessary. When a shop asks for your phone number "for offers", ask yourself whether it really needs it. Giving less data is always possible. And when someone asks you for another person's data, remember: your family's data are also to be cared for. Do not share a relative's phone number or address without their permission, just as you would not open their mail.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- A receipt, a list or an address book contains data organised in fields, rows and columns.
- The header of a table says what is in each column.
- Data must be clean and honest to be useful.
- In forms, you decide what data you give and what you do not.
In the next level we will learn to sort and classify data so that it begins to tell stories: from the most expensive to the cheapest, from A to Z, by category.
