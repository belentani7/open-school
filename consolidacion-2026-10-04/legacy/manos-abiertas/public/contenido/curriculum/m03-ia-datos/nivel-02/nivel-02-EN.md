# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 02
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Seed
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand what sorting data means and why it matters.
- Learn to sort from smallest to largest, from largest to smallest, and from A to Z.
- Classify data into categories, like organising a wardrobe or a drawer of buttons.
- Use the sort button in a spreadsheet without making mistakes.
- Discover that sorting and classifying is the first step of any analysis.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Sort | To put data in a sequence, such as from cheapest to most expensive. |
| Ascending | From smallest to largest: A to Z, 1 to 100. |
| Descending | From largest to smallest: Z to A, 100 to 1. |
| Classify | To group similar data into categories, such as fruit, bread and cleaning. |
| Category | A named group where similar data go together. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Imagine a drawer of buttons in your home. If all the buttons are mixed up, looking for a white two-hole button is an odyssey. But if someone sorted them by colour and size, finding it takes a moment. Well, sorting and classifying data is exactly that, but with numbers, names and dates instead of buttons.

In the previous level we created a table with the week's expenses: date, product, quantity and price. That table is useful, but it is as it is, with no order. If we want to know what we bought that was most expensive, we need to sort. Sorting is putting data in a sequence according to a rule. The two basic rules are: from smallest to largest (ascending) and from largest to smallest (descending). We also sort A to Z for names.

Why is sorting so useful? Think about the year's bills. Without sorting, they are a pile. Sorted by date, from January to December, they become a timeline of your expenses. Sorted by amount, from largest to smallest, they tell you at a glance which are your biggest expenses. The very same pile of papers, with a simple sort, answers different questions.

And classifying is another, different step. Classifying is grouping similar data into named categories. In the kitchen we do it without noticing: the pantry is divided into zones for pasta, pulses and tinned goods. That is classifying. In our shopping table, classifying would mean grouping products by category: fruit, bread, cleaning, pharmacy. Once classified, we can add up each group and know how much we spend on each category.

Sorting and classifying are siblings, but they are not the same thing. Sorting puts things in a line according to a rule (most expensive first). Classifying joins similar things into groups (all the fruit together). For a complete analysis, we almost always do both. First we classify into categories, then we sort each category. That is how wardrobes are organised and that is how data are organised.

And what does artificial intelligence have to do with this? Think about the photos on your phone. The phone classifies the photos automatically: faces, pets, beaches, documents. That is a machine classifying data. AI looks at each photo, finds patterns (a face has two eyes and a nose) and puts it into its category. Everything AI does began with the same human gesture of sorting and classifying.

A classic example for understanding order: the grandchildren's marks at school. If we have a list of exam marks and we sort them from largest to smallest, we know who got the highest mark and who got the lowest. If we classify them into "passed" and "failed", we know how many passed. With sorting and classification, a simple list of marks answers several questions.

Another example: the medicines in the medicine cabinet. Sorting them by expiry date (from the one that expires soonest to the one that expires latest) stops them going bad. Classifying them by use (fever, pain, plasters) makes finding them easy in an emergency. Your medicine cabinet, well sorted and classified, is a small example of data management you already practise without calling it that.

Now, how do we sort in a spreadsheet? The important thing is to select the data well. If we have a table with a header, we mark from the header box to the last box. Then we look for the button that says "sort" or "A-Z". In Google Sheets and LibreOffice Calc, that button lets you choose the column to sort by and whether it is ascending or descending. A tip: if your table has titles in the first row, tick the option "the first row is a header" so the titles do not get mixed in with the data.

A very typical beginner mistake is sorting only one column. Imagine sorting just the price column and leaving the others untouched. The result is a disaster: each price will have moved on its own and no longer matches its product. You must sort the whole table at once, selecting all the columns together. The golden rule: if you sort one column, sort its neighbours too. Data must always move as a family.

Classification also happens in daily life without a computer. When the postman delivers letters by street and number, he is sorting. When the pharmacy keeps medicines in alphabetical order, it is sorting. When you separate laundry for the washing machine by colour, you are classifying. The more aware you are of these gestures, the easier it will be for you to think with data.

In this module we use the garden metaphor: a good garden needs good seeds, well-prepared soil and steady watering. Data are the seeds; sorting and classifying is preparing the soil. Without prepared soil, the seeds do not grow. Without sorted data, analysis does not flourish. In the next levels, when we reach charts and AI, you will remember this level: it all began with sorting and classifying.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: The sock drawer
Sort a drawer of socks by pairs and colours. Then classify them into "everyday", "sport" and "warm". You have done data analysis with clothes: it is exactly the same mental process you will use with tables.

### Example 2: The sorted shopping list
Go back to your expenses table from the previous level. Sort the products from highest to lowest price. At a glance you will know which is the most expensive purchase of the week. Then classify them into fruit, bread and cleaning, and add up each group.

### Example 3: The exam marks
Write down the marks of 5 family members or friends in a list. Sort them from highest to lowest: who got the best mark? Classify them into passed and failed: how many passed?

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Open Google Sheets in your browser (https://sheets.google.com) and sign in with your account.
Step 2. Create a new sheet and write in the first row (header): "date", "product", "quantity", "price".
Step 3. Write below 6 real or invented purchases, one per row.
Step 4. Select with the mouse from the "date" box to the last box with data, selecting the whole table.
Step 5. Look in the "Data" menu for the option "Sort range" (or the A-Z button in the toolbar).
Step 6. Choose the "price" column and the option "from largest to smallest" (descending). Confirm.
Step 7. Observe: the whole table has been reordered, and each product still goes with its price. That is sorting well.
Step 8. Now add a new column on the right called "category" and write fruit, bread, cleaning or pharmacy in each row.
Step 9. Use "Sort range" again, this time by the "category" column. The products will be grouped by type. That is classifying.
Step 10. Save the sheet with a name, for example "My week's expenses".

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. What is the difference between sorting and classifying?
2. What does sorting "descending" mean?
3. Why must you select the whole table before sorting, not just one column?
4. Which button or option do we use in a spreadsheet to sort by a column?
5. How is classifying photos on your phone related to artificial intelligence?

Answers: 1. Sorting puts data in a sequence according to a rule; classifying groups similar data into categories. 2. From largest to smallest: from the highest price to the lowest, from Z to A. 3. Because if only one column moves, each piece of data separates from its row and the order loses its meaning. 4. "Sort range" in the Data menu, or the A-Z button. 5. Because the phone looks for patterns in each photo and puts it in a category, just as AI groups data.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
Sorting and classifying looks innocent, but it can be unfair if done badly. Classifying people by categories (age, neighbourhood, appearance) can lead to treating someone worse just because of their group. History is full of examples of classifications that marginalised people. When classifying data about people, always ask yourself: is this category necessary? Will it harm anyone? Classifying data about things is fine; classifying people demands great care.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
- Sorting puts data in sequence; classifying groups them into categories.
- Ascending is smallest to largest; descending is largest to smallest.
- When sorting a table, select the whole table, not a single column.
- AI classifies data automatically, just as we classify clothes or the pantry.
In the next level we will make the jump to spreadsheets: we will get to know cells, rows and columns as our working ground, and we will sort tables directly on the computer.
