# Module 3: AI Applied to Data — Level 24
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Fruit
## Estimated time: 3 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Protect personal data in daily life: passwords, two-step verification and backups.
- Understand the GDPR in simple words and apply it to our lives (Level 19).
- Recognise a scam (phishing) and know what to do if someone asks for the NIE.
- Also take care of other people's data: that of the project and that of the community.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Password | The key that protects an account; it must be long and unique. |
| Two-step verification | A second security step (a code sent to your phone) in addition to the password. |
| Backup | An extra copy of the data stored somewhere else. |
| Phishing | Fake messages that imitate banks or institutions to steal data. |
| Data breach | When private data comes to light without permission. |

## 📚 Main lesson
We already know how to collect, clean, analyse and visualise data. Today we learn the subject that protects everything before it: security. Because a perfect analysis is of little use if the data ends up in the wrong hands. Personal data is valuable: with it, someone can impersonate us, empty an account or take out loans in our name. Protecting data is protecting our life.

The first key is the password. A good password is long and unique for each account. Long, because short ones are guessed in seconds; unique, because if we use it everywhere and it is stolen once, it is already everywhere. It is like the key to the house: we would not carry the same key for the door, the car and the safe. A password manager keeps all the keys under one, and there is no need to memorise them.

The second key is two-step verification. In addition to the password, the account asks for a code that arrives on your phone. So even if someone steals the password, without your phone they cannot get in. It is like adding a second bolt. Turn on two-step verification at least on your email, because email is the front door to all the other accounts: whoever controls the email controls the passwords.

Now let us talk about the NIE and the documents. The NIE is a very sensitive personal detail: with its number and a photo of the document, a person can be impersonated. So the rule is clear: photos of documents are never sent by WhatsApp or social media. If someone asks for the NIE, you must ask what they need it for and why; the law (the GDPR) requires a clear purpose and your consent, as we saw in Level 19. A serious procedure is done through official channels, not through a message.

Another enemy is phishing: the fake messages that imitate banks, the SEPE or energy companies to steal data. "Your account will be blocked, click here", "you have been refunded, enter your password": they are traps. No legitimate institution asks for your password by message. When in doubt, do not click: call the official number yourself or open the page by typing the address by hand.

And the backup: the safety net of our data. A sheet with a year of budget, the members' list of the association or the family photos deserve an extra copy somewhere else. Keep one copy in the cloud (Drive) and another on the computer or a USB stick. If a file is deleted or the phone breaks, the copy is there. It is like having a second key at a relative's house.

And what if something has already happened? If you suspect that an account has been stolen or that your data appeared in a breach, act calmly and quickly: change the password of that account and of any that share the same one, turn on two-step verification, and notify the bank if money is involved. On the Have I Been Pwned website you can check whether your email appeared in a known leak (we saw it in Level 19).

In the immigrant context, security is especially important. The SEPE, the Social Security, the bank, the municipal register: these are institutions with valuable data. Beware of "agents" who ask for passwords or photos of documents by WhatsApp: official procedures are not done that way. And beware of offering your data to strangers who "help you with your paperwork": legitimate help never asks for the password.

The ethics of this level has two sides. One, protecting yourself: your data is yours and you decide who sees it. The other, protecting others: if you handle other people's data —the members' list, a neighbourhood survey—, you owe them the same care you want for your own. Do not share the members' list on WhatsApp, do not publish data that identifies anyone. Security is contagious: those who protect themselves protect their community.

In Level 25 we close the module. We will bring together everything learned —collect, clean, analyse, visualise, protect and decide— and learn the last skill: judgement, the ability to use data with sense and honesty. For now, spend this level putting on your locks: strong passwords, two-step verification, backups and the NIE rule. Protected data is data that is worth something.

## 💡 Practical examples
### Example 1: Luis's "bank" message
Luis receives a message from his "bank": "Your account will be blocked, confirm your password here". He does not click: he calls the bank's official number and confirms it is fake. It was phishing. He keeps the message and deletes it.

### Example 2: Ana's NIE photo
An "agent" asks Ana for a photo of her NIE by WhatsApp for "a grant". Ana stops, asks what it is for and why, and decides to take it in person to the official office. Protecting the NIE saved her from a problem.

### Example 3: Pedro's association
Pedro keeps the members' list protected with a password, makes a monthly backup and does not share it on WhatsApp. The list is protected and the association can trust him.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1. Review your passwords: do you repeat the same one in several accounts? Write it down.
Step 2. Turn on two-step verification on your email (the most important one).
Step 3. Install a free password manager (Bitwarden) and save a new, long password.
Step 4. Go to Have I Been Pwned and check whether your email appeared in a breach.
Step 5. Review the permissions of your project: is the sheet shared with someone who should not see it? Change it.
Step 6. Make a backup of your project: download a local copy.
Step 7. Review a recent suspicious message: what phishing signs did it have?
Step 8. Write your "security plan": a unique password, two-step verification, a backup and the NIE rule.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. A good password is... a) Short and easy. b) Long and unique for each account. c) Your date of birth.
2. Two-step verification... a) Is annoying and useless. b) Asks for a second step (like a code) if someone tries to get in. c) Is only for companies.
3. A message from your "bank" asking for your password... a) Is normal. b) Is a sign of phishing: do not click and call the bank. c) Reply quickly.
4. Can you send your NIE by WhatsApp? a) Yes, no problem. b) Better not: ask what it is for and hand it over through a safe channel. c) Only at night.
5. What is a backup? a) A duplicate file. b) An extra copy stored somewhere else so you do not lose the data. c) A printout.

Answers: 1-b, 2-b, 3-b, 4-b, 5-b.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- Protecting your data is a right, not a nuisance.
- The consent from Level 19 applies to you too: give your data only with a clear purpose.
- Do not share other people's data without permission: the members' list is not for WhatsApp.
- If a tool asks for more data than necessary, ask why.
- Security is shared: those who protect themselves also protect their family and their community.
