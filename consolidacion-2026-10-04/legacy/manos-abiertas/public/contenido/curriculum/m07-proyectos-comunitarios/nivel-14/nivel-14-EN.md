# Module 7: Community Projects — Level 14
## Language: EN · Difficulty: Stem
## Estimated time: 2 hours

## 🎯 Level objective
- Understand that conflicts are normal and can be useful.
- Learn the difference between the discussion that builds and the one that destroys.
- Get to know the steps to resolve a conflict calmly.
- Speak with respect, without offending or hurting.
- Reach agreements in which nobody loses their dignity.

## 📖 Essential vocabulary
| Term | Simple explanation |
|---|---|
| Conflict | The situation in which two people or groups have different opinions. |
| Discussion | The moment when those opinions are exposed, with calm or with noise. |
| Listening | Hearing the other person to the end, without interrupting, in order to understand them. |
| Agreement | The meeting point in which everyone gains a little. |
| Mediation | The help of a third person so the parties understand each other. |
| Dignity | The respect for ourselves and for others, which is never lost. |

## 📚 Main lesson
Where there are people, there are conflicts. It is inevitable, like the clouds in the sky. Two people who work together will, sooner or later, think differently about anything: the dates, the festival's music, the colour of the tarpaulins. The question is not avoiding conflicts, but knowing what to do with them.

A well-handled conflict is an opportunity. When two opinions meet, there is a chance to find a third, better than the two. The team that argues with respect decides better, because it listens to more voices. The conflict is not the project's enemy: the enemy is the way we handle it.

There is a big difference between arguing about things and arguing about people. Arguing about things is healthy: "I think the festival should be on Saturday" against "I think it should be on Sunday". Arguing about people is destructive: "you never do anything" or "you are always the same". Words attack the problem or attack the person: that is the line that separates what builds from what destroys.

The first rule for resolving a conflict is to cool down. When someone is irritated, nothing is decided: words come out like boiling water and scald. Before continuing, we stop, breathe and wait. A moment of silence, a walk around the block, a coffee drunk slowly: the body cools down and the mind clears.

The second rule is to speak one at a time. In a conflict, everyone wants to speak at the same time and nobody listens. One person speaks and the others listen to the end, without interrupting. Then the other speaks. It seems simple, but it changes everything: when we feel heard, the urge to shout disappears.

The third rule is to speak about yourself, not about the other. Instead of "you did this wrong", say "I felt bad when this happened". Speaking about yourself is not attacking: it is explaining how we feel. And the other does not feel attacked, and can listen. This small change of words is a big change of heart.

The fourth rule is to look for the problem, not the guilty one. The problem is the concrete issue: the loud music, the delay, the missing tool. The guilty one is the person. The team that looks for the problem solves it; the team that looks for the guilty creates another problem. The right question is "what happened?", not "whose fault is it?".

The fifth rule is to look for the agreement in which everyone gains a little. The agreement is not one person bending to the other: it is the meeting point. Maybe the festival will be on Saturday, but the music starts at eight and ends at twelve. Maybe the tool will have a fixed place and a person responsible. When everyone gains a little, everyone defends the agreement.

When the parties do not understand each other alone, mediation comes in: a third person, calm and neutral, who helps the parties talk. The mediator does not judge or decide: they give the floor, listen to both sides and look for common ground. Many times, an outside person sees what those involved cannot see: the cloud that covers the sun.

In mediation, as in everything, dignity is sacred. Nobody can leave a conflict humiliated, because then the conflict was not resolved: it was only hidden. The agreement that humiliates one side does not last: it explodes sooner or later. The fair agreement is the one that respects everyone's dignity, even the loser's.

After the agreement, the matter is closed. You do not hold a grudge, you do not bring it up at the next meeting. The team that forgives and moves on is a free team. Keeping offences is carrying stones in your pocket: it tires, hurts and does not let you walk. Letting the stones go is choosing the project instead of pride.

Conflicts are also prevented. Clear rules, defined roles and conversations in time avoid many clashes. When a discomfort appears, it is talked about right away, calmly and in private, before it turns into a public argument. The garden is cared for early, before the weeds grow.

In this level we will practise resolving conflicts with calm and respect. We will learn to speak about ourselves, to look for the problem and to reach agreements with dignity. We will make our team a place where differences are discussed, not where people argue.

Let us remember: a team without conflicts does not exist; a team that resolves conflicts is invincible. Arguments are like the rain: they can wet the picnic or water the garden. The difference is how we receive them.

## 💡 Practical examples
1. **The festival's music.** Two participants argue about the volume of the music. One wants it loud, the other wants it low. Instead of shouting, they speak one at a time. The first explains that loud music makes the party; the second, that he has a sick neighbour at home. The agreement: moderate volume until eleven and low afterwards. Both accept.
2. **The lost tool.** The pruning shears have disappeared. The coordinator does not look for the guilty: they look for the problem. They ask when they were used, where they were kept and who saw them last. The agreement: the shears have a fixed place and whoever uses them returns them the same day. They were never lost again.
3. **The mediation at the meeting.** Two participants argue about the fate of the vegetables: some want to sell them, others want to donate them. A mediator lets both speak and summarises: one wants money for the project, the other wants to help the families. The agreement: half is sold, half is donated. Both smile.

## 🛠️ Guided activity
Step 1: Write a recent situation in which there was a disagreement in the team.
Step 2: Separate the problem from the person: write what the concrete issue is, without names.
Step 3: Write what each side wanted, how each one sees it.
Step 4: Look for a meeting point: what can each side give a little of.
Step 5: Practise the sentence about yourself: "I felt..." instead of "you did...".
Step 6: Rehearse with a companion a conflict conversation: one speaks, the other listens to the end.
Step 7: If one day the parties do not understand each other, propose mediation: a calm, neutral person who helps them talk.

## ✍️ Self-assessment exercises
1. Conflicts in a project are: a) A sign of failure. b) Normal and even useful, if handled well. c) Always avoidable.
2. The line between arguing and hurting is: a) Speaking about the problem or speaking about the person. b) Speaking loudly or softly. c) Being right or not.
3. What is the first rule for resolving a conflict? a) Shout first. b) Cool down before continuing. c) Call the police.
4. How do we speak about ourselves without attacking the other? a) "You never do anything." b) "I felt bad when this happened." c) "You are always the same."
5. What does the mediator do? a) Decides who is right. b) Punishes the guilty. c) Helps the parties talk and find the agreement.

Answers: 1-b, 2-a, 3-b, 4-b, 5-c.

## ⚖️ Ethical dimension
- The conflict is resolved with respect: nobody is humiliated.
- You speak about the problem, never attack the person.
- Everyone's dignity is worth more than winning the argument.
- The mediator is neutral: they do not judge, they help.
- After the agreement, the matter is closed: no grudges kept.

## 🧠 Summary and bridge
Conflicts are normal and, well handled, make you grow: cooling down, speaking one at a time, speaking about yourself, looking for the problem and reaching agreements with dignity. Mediation helps when the parties do not understand each other. In the next level we will learn to do a diagnosis of the neighbourhood: getting to know the needs, the resources and the people of our community so the project serves better.
